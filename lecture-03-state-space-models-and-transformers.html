On the Tradeoffs of State-Space Models and Transformers

What a model retains, what it can recover, and where a hierarchy spends its computation

A maintenance assistant reads a continuous stream: vibration measurements, short operator notes, replacement-part serial numbers, and revisions to a safety procedure. Two queries expose different requirements. “Has the pump's vibration risen since yesterday?” asks for a persistent summary. “Which exact serial number was entered before the last shutdown?” asks for a particular historical item. A model that retains a useful trend can fail the second question; a model that stores every note can spend substantial resources reading irrelevant history.

The design problem is to choose what persists between observations. We will keep this stream and these two queries fixed while changing the representation of history. A recurrent state, a cache of contextual vectors, a hierarchy of learned chunks, and an external document store provide different interfaces to the same past. Their costs and information losses follow from those interfaces. Successful prediction, exact recall, timely updates, and safe action remain separate outcomes.

Sources and scope. The topic sequence follows Albert Gu's CS25 talk, including its academic Q&A and a separately attributed sponsor presentation. Timestamps use local machine transcription, not publisher captions. No official deck is available; the mathematical notation and diagram-dependent explanations below are checked against named original papers, rather than unseen talk animations. The maintenance stream, proofs, fixtures and exercises are original teaching constructions. Familiarity with chapter 1, matrix multiplication, probability and differentiation is assumed. Speaker biographies and promotional event logistics are omitted.

1. Classify the computation before comparing model names 1:05

Autoregressive modeling assigns a conditional distribution to the next discrete observation from an available prefix. Let $u_t\in\mathcal V$ be observation $t$ in vocabulary $\mathcal V$, and let $\theta$ denote trained parameters. The prediction is $p_\theta(u_{t+1}\mid u_1,\ldots,u_t)$. Training commonly scores observed next items; generation repeatedly samples from this distribution and appends the sample. A change of architecture changes how the prefix is represented, while the prediction objective can stay the same.

The talk groups several modern recurrent architectures under “state-space models.” This is a useful umbrella for discussing persistent compressed state, not a claim that Mamba, gated DeltaNet, test-time-training updates and every recurrent network implement one identical operator. A state-space model, in our mathematical development, will have an explicit state update and readout. We will distinguish a structured affine recurrent core from the nonlinear network surrounding it.

Gu's April 16, 2026 comparisons of Mamba-2, gated DeltaNet and the newly introduced Mamba-3 are speaker judgments at that date. They are not a present-day ranking across hardware, model sizes or downstream tasks. The March 16 Mamba-3 preprint develops improved discretization, complex-valued dynamics and multiple input/output channels (PDF pp. 1, 3–4; research extension). Those changes illustrate why “linear recurrent model” is a family description rather than a frozen implementation. A result on one configuration must name its state size, training recipe and evaluation.

The dated landscape also names xLSTM, DeltaNet and test-time-training approaches as recurrent lineages, alongside hybrid commercial examples. In this context, test-time training views a state update as online optimization of a memory objective; it need not mean retraining every language-model weight at each generated token. The named deployments motivate the discussion, but this chapter does not certify every product's current architecture. The common question is what information passes through the persistent state.

For the assistant, the useful first comparison is operational: what must be retained after the latest measurement, which operations read that retained object, and which facts can be lost? Model names answer none of these questions by themselves. The next sections establish a common accounting system before discussing empirical tradeoffs.

2. Account for one update and for an entire generation 5:23

Let $n$ be a processed sequence length and $t$ its current position. In a causal attention head, chapter 1's key and value widths are $d_k,d_v$. Each processed position contributes a key and a value to the KV cache: stored contextual vectors reused for later queries. Reading a new query against $t$ keys needs order $t d_k$ score arithmetic and $t d_v$ weighted-value arithmetic. Projection, feedforward and vocabulary-head work are additional costs, held fixed here. A cache is not a copy of an infallible database: its vectors are learned representations and its eventual query may retrieve the wrong item.

When a fresh query after each consumed observation reads positions $1,\ldots,t$, the total number of query/key pairs through position $n$ is

\[\sum_{t=1}^{n}t=\frac{n(n+1)}2.\]

The sum follows by pairing the first and last terms, then the second and next-to-last; each pair has sum $n+1$. At $n=4096$ there are 8,390,656 permitted pairs. Doubling the length approximately quadruples this total. The persistent cache itself grows linearly, not quadratically: it contains $n(d_k+d_v)$ scalar entries per head.

For an illustrative head with $d_k=d_v=64$ and two stored bytes per scalar, length 4096 requires $4096\cdot128\cdot2=1,048,576$ bytes, or 1 MiB. This counts keys and values only, for one head and one sequence; it excludes allocations, batching and other layers. It is an engineering check, not a GPU memory measurement. Exact attention kernels can reduce temporary memory without removing the pairwise operator's arithmetic.

Prefill processes an existing prompt; decode produces additional observations one at a time. For a prompt of length $p$ followed by $g$ next-item queries, the queried prefix lengths are $p,p+1,\ldots,p+g-1$. They sum to $gp+g(g-1)/2$. A 1024-item prompt and 128 queries require 139,200 prefix comparisons in this convention. Calling this entire process “quadratic” hides the distinction between the prompt and the generated tail; calling one step “linear” hides its increasing history cost.

A recurrent update instead operates on a state of fixed dimension. Its per-item cost is constant in the history length when model width, state dimension, precision and architecture are fixed. Processing $n$ items then takes order $n$ recurrent work. This says nothing about constant factors or accelerator utilization. Increasing the state as the stream grows changes the premise. Our assistant could process vibration indefinitely at a fixed update rate, but it does not follow that it can recall every serial number encountered indefinitely.

3. Derive the information path through a recurrent state 8:41

Begin with a single scalar feature $x_t\in\mathbb R$ extracted from observation $u_t$. Use column vectors locally: the state $h_t\in\mathbb R^N$ has expansion dimension $N\ge1$, the transition $A_t\in\mathbb R^{N\times N}$, and injection/readout vectors $B_t,C_t\in\mathbb R^N$. Here $A_t$ is a state transition, distinct from chapter 1's attention weight $A_{ij}$; its indices and dimensions identify the local role. Our discrete recurrent core is

\[h_t=A_t h_{t-1}+B_t x_t,\qquad y_t=C_t^\top h_t.\]

The scalar $y_t$ is a feature readout, not already a next-token probability. A complete language model uses multiple features, projections, nonlinearities and a probability head. The original Mamba paper distinguishes this structured recurrent core, its per-channel state expansion and the surrounding architecture (PDF pp. 3–5). With $d$ independently expanded channels, the state has $dN$ entries. Thus a large expanded state can have a substantial constant memory cost even while remaining independent of $n$.

A local scalar continuous-time teaching model explains one source of recurrent coefficients. Its state and fixed input $h,x$ are dimensionless real numbers; time has an arbitrary model-time unit. Over an interval of length $\Delta>0$, let $\dot h=-\lambda h+b x$ with decay rate $\lambda>0$ and coupling $b\in\mathbb R$, both in inverse model-time units, and initial value $h_{\mathrm{old}}\in\mathbb R$. Multiplying by the integrating factor $e^{\lambda \tau}$, where $\tau$ is elapsed model time, and integrating from 0 to $\Delta$ gives $h_{\mathrm{new}}=e^{-\lambda\Delta}h_{\mathrm{old}}+\frac b\lambda(1-e^{-\lambda\Delta})x$. The integration uses a constant input and constant coefficients on that interval. It does not prove the same exact formula for arbitrary continuously varying coefficients.

As $\Delta$ tends to zero, retention approaches 1 and the injection gain approaches $b\Delta$; a long interval forgets the initial state and approaches equilibrium $bx/\lambda$. For $\lambda=2$, $\Delta=(\log2)/2$ and $b=1$, the two coefficients are $1/2$ and $1/4$. Initial state 2 and input 4 produce 2, correctly preserving equilibrium. For a small interval exponent $z=\lambda\Delta$, the code computes the gain as $b\Delta[-\operatorname{expm1}(-z)/z]$, using the ratio's limit 1 when the product rounds to zero. This avoids subtracting nearly equal numbers and preserves a representable injection even when the exponent underflows. Binary exponent scaling also prevents an intermediate product from overflowing or underflowing when its final coefficient is representable. For a larger interval the code uses the equivalent $(b/\lambda)[-\operatorname{expm1}(-z)]$ with the same scaling. A nonfinite interval exponent or unrepresentable final gain is rejected; a gain smaller than the representable subnormal range can round to zero. This checks the exact scalar constant-input formula with floating-point arithmetic, not every Mamba implementation's injection rule. The Mamba-3 source explicitly discusses differing discretization approximations (PDF pp. 3–4).

Unrolling exposes how earlier observations reach a present readout. Set $h_0=0$ and define an empty matrix product as the identity $I_N$. Direct substitution gives $h_2=A_2B_1x_1+B_2x_2$. Inductively, multiplying the previous expansion by $A_t$ and adding the newest injection produces

\[h_t=\sum_{j=1}^{t}(A_tA_{t-1}\cdots A_{j+1})B_jx_j,\qquad y_t=\sum_{j=1}^{t}C_t^\top(A_t\cdots A_{j+1})B_jx_j.\]

The order matters: matrix multiplication is generally noncommutative. Each observation is written through $B_j$, transformed by every subsequent transition, and read through $C_t$. For a scalar constant transition $a=1/2$, injections equal to the input and readout 1, the inputs $(1,0,0)$ yield $(1,1/2,1/4)$. A measurement's influence decays with age. Moving the 1 to the last position yields $(0,0,1)$, so the recurrent operator retains temporal order without positional embeddings.

When $A,B,C$ are constant, the coefficient on $x_j$ depends only on lag $t-j$: $C^\top A^{t-j}B$. This is a causal convolution, meaning a lag-indexed linear filter. That equivalence is for the declared linear time-invariant core and initial-state convention. Making coefficients depend on content destroys that fixed convolution kernel. It does not destroy the explicit recurrent computation.

Selectivity makes coefficients functions of the current input representation. In a scalar teaching model, retaining the past without accepting an irrelevant measurement uses $a_t=1$ and injection 0; replacing it with a new value uses $a_t=0$ and that value as injection. The latter transition annihilates all earlier information in this coordinate. A noise packet can therefore leave the running state unchanged, while an authorized recalibration resets the baseline. Exact 0/1 gates illustrate the logic; smooth finite learned gates need not attain those endpoints.

There is an essential distinction in the word “linear.” For fixed realized coefficients, the update is affine in the previous state and linear in its injection. If coefficients depend on $x_t$, the complete input-to-output map is generally nonlinear. For example, $h_t=x_t h_{t-1}+x_t$ contains input-dependent products; superposition of input sequences fails. Scan algorithms exploit the affine state update after coefficients are computed. They do not prove that a selective model is a globally linear function of its input.

Even the fixed core's memory has a numerical horizon. In the scalar example an input's coefficient after $k$ steps is $2^{-k}$. At $k=20$ it is about $9.54\times10^{-7}$, before noise, quantization and later readout. A slow-decay coordinate retains different information from a reset coordinate. Successful long-range modeling therefore requires both enough state capacity and a learned write/retain/read strategy; state expansion alone does not specify which serial number survives.

4. Parallelize affine composition without reversing time 12:31

The apparent obstacle to training a recurrence is its serial definition: $h_t$ requires $h_{t-1}$. During a full-sequence training pass, however, each layer already has its input sequence. If its coefficients can be computed from these available representations without sequential dependence on that layer's unfinished state, every update can be represented as an affine map. Let $r_t=B_tx_t$ and denote the map $h\mapsto A_t h+r_t$ by the pair $(A_t,r_t)$.

Define chronological composition $\star$ so that the left pair acts first:

\[(A,r)\star(D,s)=(DA,Dr+s).\]

For three maps $(A,r),(D,s),(E,v)$, either grouping gives $(EDA,EDr+Es+v)$. This proves associativity with all vector/matrix domains fixed. Associativity allows a balanced tree to combine adjacent intervals; it does not allow permuting their order. In a scalar check, $(2,1)\star(3,4)=(6,7)$ whereas reversing them gives $(6,9)$. The same total transition can have a different accumulated injection.

A prefix scan returns the composition of maps 1 through $t$ for every $t$. Applying each prefix to $h_0$ produces all states. A work-efficient tree algorithm takes order $n$ compositions in total and order $\log n$ dependent levels, with enough processors and a fixed-cost composition. For dense $N\times N$ transitions, composing matrices is expensive; diagonal or scalar-times-identity structure makes the operation much cheaper. A theoretical span bound is not a measured speedup on a particular GPU.

Take scalar transitions $(1/2,1,0,1/2)$ and injections $(2,0,7,1)$. The serial states from zero are $(2,2,7,4.5)$. The first two updates combine to $(1/2,2)$; the last two combine to $(0,4.5)$. Their combined transition is zero and their total injection 4.5, agreeing with the final serial state. This calculation remains valid when a gate is zero. A shortcut that divides by a prefix product to recover interval products can fail at exactly that reset; our executable checks avoid such division.

For a generic nonlinear recurrence $h_t=\tanh(Ah_{t-1}+r_t)$, composing two steps usually cannot be represented by one pair of the same finite matrix/vector form. The sequential dependence remains unless another special structure is available. One cannot remove the nonlinear activation from an LSTM, use the affine scan, and claim to have computed the unchanged LSTM. The restriction buys parallel training while constraining the update family.

The MambaByte original spells out affine scan composition (PDF p. 4). Mamba's implementation also manages the expanded-state movement through accelerator memory; scan algebra alone does not accomplish that. A full materialization of all $n dN$ intermediate state entries can be too expensive, even though the persistent decode state has only $dN$ entries. Training activations, temporary memory, bandwidth and decode state are different quantities.

5. Understand the precise state-space/attention duality 14:39

Unrolling a scalar-times-identity transition gives a bridge to a matrix computation. Let $A_t=a_tI_N$, retain $B_t,C_t\in\mathbb R^N$, and condition on their realized values. Define a lower-triangular matrix $M\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ by $M_{tj}=0$ for $j>t$ and

\[M_{tj}=(C_t^\top B_j)\prod_{k=j+1}^{t}a_k\quad(j\le t).\]

Then the sequence of scalar outputs is $y=Mx$. The proof is exactly the recurrent expansion in section 3: scalar transitions commute with each other and can be pulled outside the dot product. Define $L_{tj}$ as the same decay product below the diagonal and zero above it. If rows of matrices $C,B\in\mathbb R^{n\times N}$ are $C_t^\top,B_t^\top$, then $M=L\odot(CB^\top)$, where $\odot$ denotes elementwise multiplication.

This is the restricted structured state-space duality used to connect a recurrent computation with a masked pairwise computation in Mamba-2/SSD (PDF pp. 5–8). There is no row softmax here. Entries of $M$ can be negative, and rows need not sum to one. For $N=1$, $B_t=C_t=1$ and $a_t=1/2$, the third row is $(1/4,1/2,1)$, whose sum is 1.75. Calling that row an attention probability distribution would be wrong.

The useful structure is in information crossing an interval boundary. Split time at position $k$. Every earlier input contributes to the future through the same $N$-dimensional state $h_k$. The block mapping inputs before that cut to later outputs factors into a map to $h_k$ and a map from $h_k$ to future outputs, so its rank is at most $N$. This statement concerns a wholly cross-cut block, not every arbitrary submatrix of a causal operator. It explains why a large pairwise matrix can admit a compact recurrent representation.

A chunked algorithm computes within-chunk interactions with matrix multiplication and represents interactions between chunks through their boundary states. Increasing chunk width increases the dense local work while reducing the number of boundary transfers. With fixed chunk width, the number of chunks is proportional to $n$; local quadratic work per chunk is a constant in $n$. Choosing chunk width proportional to $n$ changes that complexity. The appropriate size also depends on hardware; an operation count alone cannot select it.

The maintenance example now has two views of the same restricted operator. The recurrence carries a compact baseline forward. The matrix form shows how each old measurement contributes to today's baseline. That correspondence is exact for its stated coefficient sequence. For a selective model, $M$ itself changes when inputs change, so $M$ is a conditional computational view, not a fixed global linear model. Nor does the paper title establish that arbitrary softmax transformers equal arbitrary SSMs.

6. Prove a finite-memory boundary before invoking an analogy 16:32

Between two observations, a recurrent model retains state, while an attention model retains its cache. Gu's speaker-authored July 2025 essay compares these interfaces to a compressive memory and an explicit record store. This is a speaker's heuristic, not a neuroscientific equivalence. Both interfaces can be useful; a mathematical capacity argument is more precise than saying that one resembles a brain.

Suppose a deterministic state has $K$ coordinates, each with at most $2^\beta$ representable states. The whole state has at most $2^{K\beta}$ values. Here $\beta$ is a bit budget per coordinate, not an assertion that every floating-point bit configuration is valid or equally usable. Let a history consist of $m$ independent symbols from an alphabet of size $q\ge2$. There are $q^m$ histories. If a later query can demand any symbol at any position and every answer must be exact, two distinct histories cannot end in the same state: the histories differ somewhere, and that position's query would require different answers from the identical state.

Injectivity therefore requires $q^m\le2^{K\beta}$, or

\[m\log_2q\le K\beta.\]

This is an original counting theorem for the specified exact-recall task. With 64 coordinates and 8 bits each, a state has at most 512 bits. Sixty-five arbitrary bytes require 520 bits, so exact recovery of any byte from every such history is impossible through that state alone. This does not mean that every 65-byte sentence fails: natural data is structured, some histories are more likely, and an application may ask only for a summary.

A two-bit state can represent pump mode and alarm status perfectly in a toy system with four combinations. Eight equally possible three-bit serial histories cannot all fit injectively into those same four states. Preserving the alarm while losing the serial is success on one query and failure on another. More state postpones the worst-case boundary; it does not eliminate it under fixed finite precision.

Over ideal real numbers, a single coordinate can encode arbitrarily many bits in its expansion. The finite counting proof then does not apply. Such an encoding may require unbounded precision and an increasingly delicate decoder, and is not a guarantee for finite hardware. Precision or distinguishability under noise must be stated before asserting an information bound. Similarly, supplying the original log through external retrieval changes the system's retained information and invalidates a state-only lower bound.

A growing cache spends memory to preserve distinct positions, but that is not sufficient for exact recall either: projections can discard distinctions, the context can be truncated, and attention can select incorrectly. A useful evaluation therefore includes both a running-statistic task and an exact-identifier task, with controlled distractor lengths. The distinction between “can consume an indefinitely long stream” and “can recover any item from that stream” is the operational meaning of different long-context claims.

7. Allocate explicit recall within a hybrid model 20:38

A hybrid interleaves recurrent and attention layers. Recurrent layers compress and update features; attention layers expose representations of selected past positions to later queries. This combines operators inside a network. It is different from giving a model a tool that fetches an external file, although both can augment the available information. A hybrid's attention caches still grow with context unless their own policy changes.

The talk describes older recurrent-to-attention ratios near 10:1 and newer designs nearer 3:1 or 4:1. These are dated speaker summaries of particular modeling experiments, largely framed in perplexity. They are not a theorem that ten recurrent layers and one attention layer minimize a general objective, and this chapter does not certify the unseen ratio table. Layer counts also do not equal FLOPs, cache bytes, or parameter allocation: one attention layer can have different head widths and cost from one recurrent layer.

For the assistant, ask which representation reaches the recall layer. If a preceding recurrent stack has already removed a serial-number distinction at every retained position, adding attention later cannot reconstruct it from nothing. If those contextual positions retain the distinction, attention can select the relevant record. Conversely, an attention layer followed by a small state must decide which retrieved result to retain for subsequent updates. “Add a little attention” leaves these placement and information-path questions unresolved.

An original ablation would compare pure recurrence, pure attention and two hybrid placements using a common byte stream, training exposure and evaluation. Measure likelihood, identifier recovery, trend error, cache/state memory and decode latency separately. For example, a lower likelihood loss can coexist with more serial-number substitutions if serials are rare in the training objective. To claim a best hybrid, specify a decision rule weighting those outcomes and constrain a resource budget. Without that rule, a Pareto tradeoff is a more accurate description than a single winner.

8. Choose the input unit without confusing it with meaning 22:59

Attention operates on the positions it receives. A tokenizer maps text into discrete units; byte-pair encoding (BPE) constructs a vocabulary of recurring merged units rather than retaining every input byte as one position. Digital image patching likewise groups pixels before attention. In the ViT original (PDF p. 3), flattened patches are linearly projected, given positions, and processed by the transformer. These are concrete representation choices, not proofs that an individual patch or BPE token has a complete semantic interpretation.

Changing resolution changes several costs simultaneously. If a fixed byte sequence of length $n$ becomes $n/r$ coarse positions, a full pairwise score count changes from order $n^2$ to order $n^2/r^2$, while per-position projections change from order $n$ to order $n/r$ at fixed width. A fivefold reduction in positions gives a 25-fold reduction in this particular dense pair count. It does not promise 25-fold end-to-end throughput: the encoder, width changes, feedforward layers and hardware remain.

The serial-number query provides a counterexample to indiscriminate coarsening. A summary that maps “part 1037” and “part 1073” to the same generic “replacement part” feature has saved positions but erased the requested distinction. On the trend query, grouping repeated vibration samples may preserve everything needed. Good resolution is task dependent. A shorter representation is useful only when its omitted distinctions are irrelevant to the eventual query or available through another route.

A byte is one of 256 possible eight-bit units; it is not generally a Unicode character. UTF-8 encodes “é” as two bytes even though it is one code point. A grapheme displayed as one character can itself contain multiple code points. Byte-level modeling avoids a learned subword vocabulary but still chooses an encoding and normalization policy. A repeated measurement serialized with different punctuation or numeric precision also changes the byte stream. An end-to-end model does not remove the data pipeline.

The talk's argument for learned input abstraction is a research motivation: fixed segmentation can impose awkward boundaries, and learned compression may adapt them. It is not an established implication that every tokenizer harms every task. Later contextual layers combine information across input positions, so an attention vector is not forever restricted to the meaning of its original character. Keep that qualification when using the heuristic that meaningful units are easier to attend to.

9. Compare likelihood on a common byte denominator 26:41

Different input units produce different denominators in mean loss. Let $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{sum}}=-\log p_\theta(s)$ be total natural-log loss for the scored representation of a fixed text $s$, and let $n_{\mathrm{byte}}>0$ be the number of encoded bytes assigned to the same scored content. Define bits per byte (BPB) as

\[\mathrm{BPB}=\frac{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{sum}}}{n_{\mathrm{byte}}\log2}.\]

The factor $\log2$ converts nats to bits. In a teaching example, a four-byte ASCII string has probability $1/16$. A single coarse token with that probability has token perplexity 16. Four byte tokens, each with conditional probability $1/2$, have the same sequence probability and token perplexity 2. Both give BPB 1. The token perplexities differ because their unit differs; neither implies a factual or recall advantage.

BPB aligns a denominator, not the entire experimental protocol. Models must score the same content, with compatible beginning/end treatment and context, to make that alignment meaningful. Different segmentation models need not assign equal probabilities to the same string. A finite model vocabulary can also impose a particular segmentation rather than marginalizing every possible segmentation. BPB does not automatically correct differing data, padding, truncation or evaluation windows.

MambaByte establishes token-free recurrent modeling on 8192-byte sequences and distinguishes parameter-matched from compute-matched comparisons (PDF pp. 1, 4–5; empirical results). The curve shown in Gu's discussion is a later reproduction in H-Net's FineWeb-Edu setting, not the original PG19 experiment. It must not inherit the original paper's compute multiplier merely because both use the name MambaByte.

The H-Net original describes this reproduction with a 1024-position sliding-window attention baseline and a global attention baseline using roughly twice the FLOPs (PDF pp. 11–13). Its plotted training curves favor the recurrent byte model over these isotropic baselines over the shown training regime. The final Large-scale table nevertheless rounds both MambaByte and global LlamaByte to 0.845 BPB, while sliding-window LlamaByte is 0.859. The table and curve answer different questions; a statement that one is strictly better at every endpoint would exceed this evidence.

For maintenance logs, preserve the byte stream, context length, total bytes seen and FLOPs-per-byte calculation when changing the backbone. Report exact-serial and trend outcomes beside BPB. If one model sees twice as much data, a lower BPB is a result for that recipe, not a controlled architectural effect. The numerical lab tests the unit conversion only; it does not train or reproduce any of these neural benchmarks.

10. Turn abstraction heuristics into testable hypotheses 29:33

Two useful questions from the talk are whether it is worth retaining every input position and whether selecting one such position would help a query. In the assistant's log, a particular serial-number field has a natural retrieval role; a single vibration character or a redundant punctuation byte often does not. A compressed running feature may be more useful than separately attending to every digit of every sample. This motivates a representation, but it is not a formal characterization of what softmax attention can learn.

Hard attention selects an individual position; soft attention mixes positions by weights. Soft attention can approximate a concentrated selection when one logit dominates, but it can also combine several items. Subsequent representations can already summarize a prefix. “This raw unit has little standalone meaning” therefore does not imply “a transformer cannot use it.” In genomics, even one nucleotide change can be consequential. Any abstraction heuristic must preserve the fine distinctions required by its task.

An original test adds distractor samples while holding relevant events fixed. Train or evaluate on paired streams with identical authorized part changes and trend segments, then insert irrelevant noise at different rates. Compare a cache model and a compressive model on exact recall and running summaries. If compression helps only when distractors rise, that supports a noise-filtering explanation. If learned hierarchy helps with no distractors, its effect may involve representing useful multi-item patterns. Neither observation alone proves the broad “meaningful token” thesis.

The transcript mentions a graph assigning about 10% of early Mamba applications to language. Its collection method and denominator are not established by the available graph description, so no usage-share statistic is adopted here. What remains is the dated qualitative observation that recurrent models were being explored across modalities. A citation count or a paper-category pie chart would not directly measure deployment, accuracy or economic usefulness.

11. Learn a boundary without inventing a vocabulary table 33:43

A hierarchical network, or H-Net, places a lightweight sequence encoder before a main network and a decoder after it. A learned routing module reduces the number of representations sent to the main network. The decoder returns to the original prediction resolution. Hwang, Wang and Gu's H-Net original defines the actual routing and reconstruction mechanisms (PDF pp. 5–10), supplementing the talk's high-level description.

Let the encoder's causal output after byte $t$ be the row vector $e_t\in\mathbb R^d$. “Causal” means it depends only on bytes $1,\ldots,t$. Define learned projections $W_{\mathrm{rq}},W_{\mathrm{rk}}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ and rows $q_t=e_tW_{\mathrm{rq}}$, $k_t=e_tW_{\mathrm{rk}}$. For $t>1$, suppose both norms in the denominator are nonzero. A router score and its hard decision are

\[\rho_t=\frac12\left(1-\frac{q_tk_{t-1}^\top}{\|q_t\|_2\|k_{t-1}\|_2}\right),\qquad b_t=\mathbf1\{\rho_t\ge1/2\},\qquad \rho_1=b_1=1.\]

Here $\|\cdot\|_2$ is Euclidean norm, $b_t$ is a boundary indicator, and $\mathbf1$ is 1 when its condition holds and 0 otherwise. The cosine lies in $[-1,1]$ by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, so $\rho_t\in[0,1]$. Parallel unit vectors give score 0; perpendicular vectors give $1/2$; opposite unit vectors give 1. A zero vector requires an explicit normalization convention in an implementation: the displayed quotient is undefined there. We do not silently extend this formula to zero norms.

The selected positions, in increasing order, are $s_1,\ldots,s_m$, where $b_{s_j}=1$ and $s_1=1$. Downsampling retains $e_{s_j}$; it does not average every byte in a yet-unfinished segment. These are already contextual representations. A vector at a boundary can summarize the prefix that has been observed at that position. “Chunk” therefore names a learned processing unit, not a guarantee that a selected vector is a lossless representation of all raw bytes assigned to a linguistic word.

A main network sees the shorter sequence of continuous vectors. It does not need an embedding-table entry for every possible new string. The first byte lookup has 256 choices; later vectors lie in a learned continuous representation space, which permits context-dependent boundaries and representations for unseen combinations. This answers the dynamic-vocabulary question without postulating an infinite discrete table.

The actual static boundary examples in the primary paper show selected spaces, word fragments and larger groupings (PDF p. 15, Figure 4; qualitative empirical evidence). We inspected that original figure, not the talk's training animation. A few examples do not establish that every boundary is linguistically correct or universally semantic. For the maintenance assistant, a boundary around a serial-number field is useful only if the representation preserves its exact digits or a separate fine-resolution route retains them.

12. Return coarse information to fine positions causally 37:04

Suppose the causal main network produces a column vector $z_j\in\mathbb R^{d'}$ from selected inputs 1 through $j$, with $d'$ its output width. Let $\bar z_0=0$ and use the selected confidence $\rho_{s_j}$ to smooth its output:

\[\bar z_j=\rho_{s_j}z_j+(1-\rho_{s_j})\bar z_{j-1}.\]

A confident boundary near 1 mostly accepts the new coarse feature; a less confident selected boundary blends it with the previous feature. The coefficients sum to one, so this particular smoothing step is a convex combination. That property belongs to the EMA, not to every SSM dependency matrix. It does not make the feature a calibrated probability or a safety assessment.

For raw position $t$, let $j(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}b_i$ be the number of boundaries already observed. Causal upsampling returns $\bar z_{j(t)}$. It repeats the most recent available coarse output until another selected position appears. With scalar boundary features $(2,6)$ and confidences $(1,3/4)$, the smoothed values are 2 and $3/4\cdot6+1/4\cdot2=5$. For boundary indicators $(1,0,1,0)$, the expanded values are $(2,2,5,5)$.

To prove causality, consider any two byte sequences identical through position $t$. Their causal encoders have identical $e_1,\ldots,e_t$. The router compares only current and previous representations, so boundary decisions through $t$ match. The same selected prefix reaches the causal main network, yielding the same $z_1,\ldots,z_{j(t)}$ and smoothed prefix. Upsampling at $t$ uses the same index. Thus its result at $t$ is unchanged by altering bytes after $t$. A causal fine-resolution decoder and a next-byte head preserve this property. The proof requires every component to respect the stated prefix condition.

The failure of a plausible shortcut makes that hypothesis concrete. Group scalar inputs $(0,10)$ into one chunk, average it to 5, and expand the average to both positions. At the first position the representation is 5 even though only 0 has been observed. Changing the future 10 to 20 changes that first result to 10. This procedure leaks future information for next-byte prediction after the first input. It may be legitimate for a noncausal classification task, but it computes a different operator.

The original H-Net equations and inference procedure retain selected encoder positions and repeat already available coarse features (PDF pp. 6–10). A prediction after input byte $t$ scores byte $t+1$; it must not ingest that target to improve the representation at $t$. The word “summarize a chunk” can obscure this indexing. Our executable fixture explicitly tests prefix invariance rather than relying on a verbal assurance that the network is autoregressive.

H-Net also carries a fine-resolution encoder-to-decoder residual projection. The coarse path can therefore compress aggressively while the decoder still receives local detail. This is important for a one-digit difference in a serial field: the hierarchy's main sequence is not the system's only information route. The design does not contradict the earlier state-only capacity theorem, whose premise excluded other retained or externally supplied information.

13. Separate a discrete forward decision from its training surrogate 40:26

The threshold decision $b_t$ jumps when $\rho_t$ crosses $1/2$. Away from that threshold, the derivative of the hard decision with respect to its score is zero; at the threshold it is undefined. Backpropagating through a naïve hard selector gives little useful signal about whether moving a boundary would improve the next-byte objective. H-Net combines smoothing with a straight-through estimator and a compression regularizer (original PDF pp. 7–9). These are training mechanisms, not a proof that every desired segmentation is recovered.

Define a decision-confidence scalar $c_t=\rho_t$ for a retained position and $c_t=1-\rho_t$ otherwise. Its straight-through factor is $\mathrm{ST}(c_t)=c_t+\operatorname{stopgrad}(1-c_t)$. The operator $\operatorname{stopgrad}$ keeps its argument's forward value and supplies zero backward derivative. Thus the factor equals 1 in the forward computation, while the prescribed backward derivative with respect to $c_t$ is 1. It is inaccurate to say this factor attenuates a forward value by $c_t$; the confidence-weighted smoothing is a separate operation.

This backward prescription is a surrogate for learning discrete routing. It is not the ordinary derivative of the forward constant 1, nor an automatically unbiased estimator of a discrete objective's gradient. Its usefulness is an empirical property of the optimization recipe. The original fixture below implements the causal forward smoothing and expansion only; it does not pretend to implement the trained router, its surrogate backward pass or the full H-Net optimizer.

Compression has its own tradeoff. Retaining everything removes much of the computational saving; retaining almost nothing can discard predictive information. Let $F=n^{-1}\sum_tb_t$ be the selected fraction and $G=n^{-1}\sum_t\rho_t$ the mean router score. For target compression $R>1$, the paper's ratio penalty is

\[\mathcal L_{\mathrm{ratio}}= \frac{R}{R-1}\left((R-1)FG+(1-F)(1-G)\right).\]

Here $R$ is a target raw-position-to-selected-position ratio, not the realized ratio $1/F$. On the diagonal $F=G=f$, the expression is $\frac{R}{R-1}(Rf^2-2f+1)$. Its derivative is $\frac{2R}{R-1}(Rf-1)$ and its second derivative is positive. The diagonal minimum is at $f=1/R$ with value 1. That calculation does not prove a global unconstrained lower bound of 1: at $R=2$, $F=0.6,G=0.4$ give 0.96.

The off-diagonal example is compatible with thresholded scores: among ten positions, use one score 1, five scores $1/2$, and four scores $1/8$. Six positions are retained and the score sum is 4. The diagonal minimization is over abstract $f\in[0,1]$; a finite sequence with its first boundary forced has $F,G\ge1/n$, so some targets may be unattainable. Holding hard decisions fixed, the derivative with respect to $G$ is $\frac{R}{R-1}(RF-1)$. It pushes mean scores down when too many positions are selected and up when too few are selected. Subsequent crossings change $F$ discretely. This is feedback toward a rate, not a differentiable identity forcing an exact compression factor.

A two-level hierarchy can target $R_0$ and $R_1$, but its actual inner length depends on both learned selected fractions. Its main network may itself be another H-Net. For the assistant, the first level could form fields and local events, and a second could form intervals of operation; those are proposed interpretations, not supervised labels guaranteed by the architecture. The original static figure supports qualitative grouping, while quantitative predictive and resource measurements must decide whether those groups are useful.

Let a raw sequence have length $n$ and realized coarse length approximately $n/r$. A main attention network's pair term changes from order $n^2$ to order $(n/r)^2$, but outer-network work remains. Doubling compute with $\alpha=0.06$ multiplies predicted perplexity by $2^{-0.06}\approx0.959264$. This is an in-range empirical fit, not an asymptotic theorem: extending it indefinitely predicts perplexity below 1, impossible for a normalized distribution scored with nonnegative negative-log loss. A broader model would need a floor or another regime.

The dnaHNet paper reports a 3.89-fold FLOP reduction for its 218M configuration against a 166M StripedHyena2 configuration at one million nucleotides (PDF p. 6). 17. Q&A: distinguish a truncated gradient from impossible learning 54:31

The academic discussion asks how recurrence, accelerator design and backpropagation constrain one another. Backpropagation through time differentiates a recurrent computation by following its dependency graph over successive positions. Even if inference retains a fixed state, training may need activations, checkpoints or recomputation to supply gradients. Constant persistent inference memory does not imply constant training memory.

In a scalar fixed recurrence $h_t=ah_{t-1}+x_t$ with loss $\ell=h_n^2/2$, the derivative with respect to an earlier input is $h_n a^{n-j}$. The factor arises from the product of transition derivatives along the path; it can decay, persist or grow. When $a=1$, $x_1=1$ and all later inputs are zero, $h_n=1$ and the derivative with respect to $x_1$ is 1. A computational graph that detaches a later state from all earlier inputs returns zero through that detached path, even though the mathematical full-sequence derivative is nonzero.

Truncated BPTT stops such gradients beyond a chosen horizon. That removes the direct training signal through this path, not every possible way to learn long dependencies. Shared parameters can learn from shorter examples; curricula, auxiliary losses, checkpointing and other algorithms change the situation. Gu's concern about memory-limited training is a research judgment. It should not be restated as a theorem that any dependency longer than a stored activation window is universally unlearnable.

The affine scan restrictions likewise couple expressivity to computation. A more general state-dependent nonlinear update may be attractive for modeling but harder to parallelize. Changing hardware or learning algorithms could alter which restrictions are worthwhile. The talk discusses alternatives to current backpropagation and “true recurrence” as promising directions, not completed demonstrations that an alternative solves the assistant's long-term memory problem.

22. Q&A: interpret a dependency map at the right level 1:05:04

A recurrent operator can be unrolled into pairwise coefficients such as $M_{tj}$ in section 5. Displaying those coefficients makes temporal influence inspectable even when the implementation uses states. The coefficients can be diffuse, signed or large. They are not normalized attention probabilities, and their visualization alone does not prove which observation caused an application's final answer.

For a selective operator, there is another distinction: coefficients evaluated on one input are not generally its total input Jacobian. In a two-step teaching recurrence, let $h_1=x_1$ and $h_2=x_2h_1+x_2$. At a realized input, $h_2=(x_2,1)(x_1,x_2)^\top$, so the conditional coefficient on the second input is 1. Differentiating the whole map instead gives $\partial h_2/\partial x_2=x_1+1$. At $(x_1,x_2)=(2,3)$ the output is 9 and the derivative is 3. The missing term comes from changing the transition itself.

For a maintenance forecast, a map can generate hypotheses about which past measurement is read or retained. 24. Executable checks: composition, causality and comparable units

The downloadable standard-library Python program implements scalar affine recurrence by a serial loop, a balanced prefix scan and direct unrolling. It also implements a forward-only selected-boundary smoother/expander, a stable scalar discretization check, BPB conversion and the ratio penalty. These are original finite models of the mathematical hinges above. They do not train Mamba, H-Net or a retrieval model, implement a GPU kernel, or establish a benchmark advantage.

Inputs are nonempty equally sized sequences of finite real numbers representable as Python floats; booleans and unsupported types are excluded. Scores lie in $[0,1]$, with the first boundary score exactly 1. Byte counts are positive integers. The code converts supported numeric inputs to binary64 and raises ValueError for invalid inputs or nonfinite computed intermediates. Finite inputs can overflow during composition, so passing the initial domain check is not a promise that every subsequent operation is representable.

The scan is a work-efficient tree expressed by ordinary Python loops, so this implementation executes serially. Its algebra exposes parallel dependency levels but supplies no accelerator timing claim. Different floating-point association can change rounding; agreement checks use a numerical tolerance rather than asserting bitwise associativity. The chunk test assumes supplied causal encoder values and router scores. It tests the forward operator's prefix property, not whether an arbitrary learned encoder is causal.

The program writes only its printed evidence. It rejects ragged lengths, zero bytes, scores outside the allowed range, huge integers that cannot become floats, and overflowing products. A future-value perturbation preserves the earlier expanded outputs; a reset transition is checked without dividing by zero. The exact output below is captured from the actual downloadable program.

"""Original finite checks for recurrence, routing and likelihood units."""
import math


def finite_real(value, name="value"):
    if isinstance(value, bool) or not isinstance(value, (int, float)):
        raise ValueError(name + " must be a real int or float")
    try:
        result = float(value)
    except (OverflowError, ValueError) as error:
        raise ValueError(name + " is not representable") from error
    if not math.isfinite(result):
        raise ValueError(name + " must be finite")
    return result


def real_sequence(values, name):
    if not isinstance(values, (list, tuple)) or not values:
        raise ValueError(name + " must be a nonempty list or tuple")
    return [finite_real(value, name) for value in values]


def coefficients(transitions, injections):
    a = real_sequence(transitions, "transition")
    r = real_sequence(injections, "injection")
    if len(a) != len(r):
        raise ValueError("coefficient lengths must agree")
    return a, r


def compose(earlier, later):
    """Chronological affine composition; later acts after earlier."""
    if not all(isinstance(pair, (list, tuple)) and len(pair) == 2
               for pair in (earlier, later)):
        raise ValueError("each affine pair must have two entries")
    a, r = (finite_real(value) for value in earlier)
    d, s = (finite_real(value) for value in later)
    return finite_real(d*a, "transition product"), finite_real(d*r+s)


def serial_states(transitions, injections, initial=0):
    a, r = coefficients(transitions, injections)
    state = finite_real(initial, "initial state")
    outputs = []
    for transition, injection in zip(a, r):
        state = finite_real(transition*state + injection, "state")
        outputs.append(state)
    return outputs


def scan_states(transitions, injections, initial=0):
    """Blelloch-style tree: O(n) composition work, serial Python loops."""
    a, r = coefficients(transitions, injections)
    initial = finite_real(initial, "initial state")
    pairs = list(zip(a, r))
    size = 1
    while size < len(pairs):
        size *= 2
    tree = pairs + [(1.0, 0.0)]*(size - len(pairs))
    stride = 2
    while stride <= size:
        for right in range(stride - 1, size, stride):
            left = right - stride//2
            tree[right] = compose(tree[left], tree[right])
        stride *= 2
    tree[-1] = (1.0, 0.0)
    stride = size
    while stride >= 2:
        for right in range(stride - 1, size, stride):
            left = right - stride//2
            old_left, prefix = tree[left], tree[right]
            tree[left] = prefix
            tree[right] = compose(prefix, old_left)
        stride //= 2
    outputs = []
    for prefix, pair in zip(tree, pairs):
        transition, injection = compose(prefix, pair)
        outputs.append(finite_real(transition*initial + injection))
    return outputs


def unrolled_states(transitions, injections, initial=0):
    """Independent quadratic expansion; products never divide by gates."""
    a, r = coefficients(transitions, injections)
    initial = finite_real(initial, "initial state")
    outputs = []
    for last in range(len(a)):
        contribution = 0.0
        product = 1.0
        for first in range(last, -1, -1):
            contribution = finite_real(contribution + product*r[first])
            product = finite_real(product*a[first])
        outputs.append(finite_real(contribution + product*initial))
    return outputs


def zoh_coefficients(decay_rate, step, coupling):
    """Scalar constant-input formula, including underflowed interval exponents.

    Use a limiting ratio near zero and binary scaling for the gain products.
    A final unrepresentable coefficient or nonfinite exponent is rejected.
    """
    rate = finite_real(decay_rate, "decay rate")
    step = finite_real(step, "step")
    coupling = finite_real(coupling, "coupling")
    if rate <= 0 or step < 0:
        raise ValueError("positive rate and nonnegative step required")
    exponent = finite_real(rate*step, "interval exponent")
    transition = math.exp(-exponent)
    if step == 0 or coupling == 0:
        return transition, 0.0
    if exponent <= 1:
        ratio = 1.0 if exponent == 0 else -math.expm1(-exponent)/exponent
        c_mantissa, c_power = math.frexp(coupling)
        s_mantissa, s_power = math.frexp(step)
        r_mantissa, r_power = math.frexp(ratio)
        mantissa = c_mantissa*s_mantissa*r_mantissa
        power = c_power+s_power+r_power
    else:
        c_mantissa, c_power = math.frexp(coupling)
        f_mantissa, f_power = math.frexp(-math.expm1(-exponent))
        d_mantissa, d_power = math.frexp(rate)
        mantissa = c_mantissa*f_mantissa/d_mantissa
        power = c_power+f_power-d_power
    try:
        gain = finite_real(math.ldexp(mantissa, power), "injection gain")
    except OverflowError:
        raise ValueError("injection gain is not representable") from None
    return transition, gain


def causal_expand(values, scores):
    """Identity main network, selected EMA, latest-boundary expansion."""
    values = real_sequence(values, "encoder value")
    scores = real_sequence(scores, "boundary score")
    if len(values) != len(scores):
        raise ValueError("value and score lengths must agree")
    if scores[0] != 1 or any(not 0 <= score <= 1 for score in scores):
        raise ValueError("scores in [0, 1], first score exactly 1")
    latest = 0.0
    selected = []
    expanded = []
    for position, (value, score) in enumerate(zip(values, scores)):
        if score >= 0.5:
            latest = finite_real(score*value + (1-score)*latest)
            selected.append(position)
        expanded.append(latest)
    return selected, expanded


def bits_per_byte(probabilities, byte_count):
    probabilities = real_sequence(probabilities, "probability")
    if (isinstance(byte_count, bool) or not isinstance(byte_count, int)
            or byte_count <= 0):
        raise ValueError("byte count must be a positive integer")
    count = finite_real(byte_count, "byte count")
    if any(not 0 < value <= 1 for value in probabilities):
        raise ValueError("probabilities must lie in (0, 1]")
    loss = finite_real(math.fsum(-math.log(p) for p in probabilities))
    return finite_real(loss/count/math.log(2))


def ratio_loss(selected_fraction, mean_score, target):
    fraction = finite_real(selected_fraction, "selected fraction")
    mean = finite_real(mean_score, "mean score")
    target = finite_real(target, "target ratio")
    if not (0 <= fraction <= 1 and 0 <= mean <= 1 and target > 1):
        raise ValueError("fractions in [0, 1] and target greater than 1")
    return finite_real(target*fraction*mean
                       + target/(target-1)*(1-fraction)*(1-mean))

25. Six exercises with complete solutions

Exercise 1 · History cost and finite recall

Count the causal query/key pairs through length 4096 and the cache bytes for one head with key/value widths 64 and two bytes per scalar. Compare a 64-channel recurrent state with expansion 64 at the same storage precision. Separately prove that 64 eight-bit coordinates cannot guarantee arbitrary exact recall from every 65-byte history. Why is it incorrect to infer that this recurrent model must fail every trend prediction?

Solution

The pair count is $4096\cdot4097/2=8,390,656$. The head cache contains $4096(64+64)$ scalars and takes 1,048,576 bytes, or 1 MiB. The illustrative recurrent state has $64\cdot64=4096$ entries and takes 8192 bytes, or 8 KiB. This compares only these retained objects, not equally capable full architectures or total training memory. The recurrent example here has 16-bit storage; the separate recall question stipulates eight-bit coordinates and must not silently borrow its capacity.

Under the latter stipulation there are at most $2^{512}$ states and $256^{65}=2^{520}$ histories. Two histories must collide. They differ at some position; asking for that byte requires distinct answers, but identical state and query cannot produce both. The contradiction proves that no deterministic state-only decoder can succeed on every such query. A trend query can depend on far less than all history bits; a four-state pump-status machine may solve its task exactly. The theorem concerns worst-case arbitrary recall under fixed finite precision, not typical natural streams, approximate recovery or systems with an external record path.

Exercise 2 · Scan, reset and a signed dependency map

Prove associativity of chronological affine composition. Compute states from zero for transitions $(1/2,1,0,1/2)$ and injections $(2,0,7,1)$ using two length-two chunks. Then form the first three rows of the scalar recurrent dependency matrix for constant transition $1/2$ and unit injection/readout. Is this matrix an attention probability map? What changes if the third readout is -1?

Solution

For pairs $(A,r),(D,s),(E,v)$, composing either way gives transition $EDA$ and injection $EDr+Es+v$. This follows from distributivity and the associativity of matrix multiplication, with the chronological order unchanged. The first chunk composes to $(1/2,2)$; the second composes $(0,7)$ with $(1/2,1)$, giving $(0,4.5)$. Applied after the first chunk it still returns 4.5 because its transition is zero. The individual states are 2, 2, 7 and 4.5. A ratio of prefix products would divide by zero at the reset and is unnecessary.

The three-row matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\1/2&1&0\\1/4&1/2&1\end{pmatrix}$. Its last row sums to 1.75, so it is not a normalized attention row. A third readout of -1 negates that last row; its entries then become negative. The matrix correctly expresses this fixed core's sequence map. An input-dependent coefficient model needs additional derivative terms when inputs are perturbed, and neither matrix visualization by itself is a causal explanation of a neural decision.

Exercise 3 · A comparable likelihood unit

A six-byte ASCII string receives total probability $1/64$. One tokenizer represents it by one token; another represents it by three tokens with probabilities $1/2,1/4,1/8$. Compute both token perplexities and BPB. Explain what changes when a string contains “é,” and list three protocol differences that BPB alone cannot repair.

Solution

The first tokenizer has token loss $\log64$ and perplexity 64. The second has the same summed loss but mean loss $\log64/3=\log4$, giving perplexity 4. For both stipulated models, dividing total loss by six bytes and $\log2$ gives BPB 1. They were assigned the same string probability in this exercise; a different segmentation model need not actually assign it that probability. The denominator conversion is not an invariance theorem for trained language models.

UTF-8 “é” occupies two bytes while containing one code point. A byte count must come from the specified encoding, not a count of displayed characters. Normalization can change the byte stream, and end markers require an agreed scoring rule. BPB cannot by itself repair different held-out text, different available context or window stride, or different training exposure/compute. Even a controlled BPB advantage measures predictive distribution fit; exact serial recovery, compatibility and safe action require their own tests.

Exercise 4 · Causal expansion and a surrogate gradient

Given causal scalar encoder values $(2,99,6,-99)$ and boundary scores $(1,0.1,0.75,0.1)$, select at threshold $1/2$, smooth and expand. Change the last value to 1000. Which outputs stay fixed? Contrast this with averaging $(0,10)$ and copying the average to both positions. Compute the forward value and prescribed derivative of $\mathrm{ST}(c)=c+\operatorname{stopgrad}(1-c)$.

Solution

The selected zero-based positions are 0 and 2, with values 2 and 6. First smoothing gives 2; the second gives $0.75\cdot6+0.25\cdot2=5$. Expansion gives $(2,2,5,5)$. The changed final value is not selected, so all four outputs happen to stay fixed in this supplied-value fixture. In general causality promises only that outputs before a changed future input stay fixed; a selected later input or the fine decoder can change later outputs. This fixture does not establish invariance to every possible learned representation change.

Averaging $(0,10)$ gives $(5,5)$ after repetition. Changing the second input to 20 gives $(10,10)$ and changes the first output using unseen information. That operator is unsuitable for causal prediction at the first position. Finally ST has forward value 1 and prescribed derivative 1 with respect to $c$. The ordinary derivative of its forward constant would be zero; the stop-gradient rule intentionally supplies a surrogate. It need not be unbiased and does not guarantee an optimal segmentation. Prefix causality and optimization quality are distinct questions.

Exercise 5 · Rate regularization and resource saving

Derive the ratio-loss minimum under $F=G=f$ for target $R>1$. Give a threshold-compatible example with $R=2$ and loss below 1. Suppose an illustrative uncompressed computation has outer cost 100 and main pair cost 3600; compression by six leaves the outer cost fixed and divides only the main pair cost by 36. Compute the total saving and explain why it does not establish runtime improvement.

Solution

On the diagonal the loss is $\frac{R}{R-1}(Rf^2-2f+1)$. Its derivative vanishes at $f=1/R$ and its second derivative $2R^2/(R-1)$ is positive, so that is the unique diagonal minimum with value 1. With ten scores $(1,1/2,1/2,1/2,1/2,1/2,1/8,1/8,1/8,1/8)$, six are retained and the average score is 0.4. Thus $F=0.6,G=0.4$ and the $R=2$ loss is $2(0.24+0.24)=0.96$. A diagonal result cannot be promoted to an unrestricted global lower bound.

The original total is 3700 and the compressed total is $100+3600/36=200$, a factor of 18.5 rather than 36. This arithmetic assumes exactly the named cost terms and unchanged constants; widths, routing, residual work or variable compression could change them. A GPU's throughput also depends on utilization, bandwidth, batching and kernels. FLOPs describe an accounting model, not a stopwatch. A measured runtime claim needs named hardware and workload as well as the prediction/recall outcomes.

Exercise 6 · Interpretability and a decision-relevant ablation

For $h_1=x_1$, $h_2=x_2h_1+x_2$, calculate the output and total derivative with respect to $x_2$ at $(2,3)$. Compare it with the frozen recurrent coefficient. Design a comparison for pure recurrence, attention and a hierarchy on maintenance logs that could distinguish summary quality from exact recall. State why two CLIP towers do not imply incompatible embedding spaces and why retrieved visual similarity cannot authorize opening a valve.

Solution

The output is 9. Holding realized coefficients fixed gives conditional row $(3,1)$, so its second coefficient is 1. Differentiating the full input-dependent expression $x_1x_2+x_2$ gives $x_1+1=3$. The changed input alters the transition as well as the direct injection. A plotted fixed-coefficient map therefore omits a real contribution to the total derivative; even that derivative remains a statement about the model, not about a physical intervention.

Use identical serialized streams and held-out machines, with explicit training bytes, precision, parameter/FLOP budgets and repeated seeds. Independently vary distractor rate and identifier-query distance. Score running-trend error, exact serial recovery, BPB, realized selected fractions, retained memory and actual decode latency. Compare a hierarchy with and without its fine residual or external record path to locate the information route. Report uncertainty and per-task outcomes; a BPB winner need not meet a hard recall constraint.

CLIP's separate image/text encoders are jointly trained with a paired objective into a shared embedding space. Distinct networks can output aligned coordinates, though empirical modality gaps remain possible. A high image/page cosine score supplies candidates, not proof of an applicable procedure. Opening the valve additionally requires a current authoritative revision, verified lockout and compatibility, and operator authorization. Neither an architecture choice nor a retrieval score establishes those preconditions.
