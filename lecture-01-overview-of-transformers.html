CS25: Transformers United V6 · Chapter 1 · Spring 2026

Overview of Transformers

From predicting a token to building a system whose evidence and actions can be checked

A maintenance technician asks an offline assistant, in English mixed with Spanish, whether a replacement valve fits a machine and which steps make installation safe. An old manual permits one procedure; the revised manual requires an additional lockout. A fluent answer that combines the old procedure with the new part number can be more dangerous than an explicit refusal. The assistant must understand the question, select the applicable evidence, explain the procedure, and leave physical action to an authorized operator.

This one task separates several questions often collapsed into “make the model smarter.” How does a token obtain information from another token? What does a training objective reward? Where should scarce training data go? What can retrieval add? Does an attractive explanation establish a correct decision? Transformers provide a useful architecture for representing sequences, but the assistant's reliability also depends on data, inference, memory, evidence policy, and the boundary between a proposal and an action.

Source and prerequisites. The topic sequence follows Steven Y. Feng and Karan Singh's CS25 V6 overview and its official slides. Navigation times come from local machine transcription, not publisher captions. Linear algebra, elementary probability, derivatives, and the distinction between a function and its parameters are assumed. The maintenance case, numerical fixtures, derivations, and exercises are original teaching constructions. Research results have their own named sources and limits. Course administration and speaker biographies are omitted.

1. Turn a training example into a prediction problem 6:52

Lecture-derived spine: engineered features, supervised learning, self-supervision, and language/vision examples. Original development: the assistant's conditional-probability model and loss calculation.

A learned model is a parameterized function whose parameters are adjusted using examples. In supervised learning, an example pairs an input with a desired output supplied by an annotation or measurement. An older sentiment classifier might first count selected positive and negative words, then fit a classifier on those counts. A deep model instead learns intermediate representations from less processed input. “Raw” remains relative: digital text has already been encoded, and photographs have already passed through a sensor and preprocessing.

Self-supervised learning constructs a prediction target from the data itself. It removes the need for a person to label every example; it does not remove choices about the objective, source selection, filtering, or evaluation. An image can supply a hidden patch to reconstruct. A text sequence can supply its next token. These are different objectives even when their networks share transformer components. A model that reconstructs a photograph is not thereby a next-token language model.

A token is an element of a fixed discrete vocabulary $\mathcal V$, produced by a specified tokenizer. It might be a word, a subword fragment, punctuation, or a byte-based unit. Let $u_1,\ldots,u_T\in \mathcal V$ be a sequence of length $T$, and let $u_{<t}=(u_1,\ldots,u_{t-1})$ be its prefix. The trainable parameters are $\theta$. A causal language model supplies a probability distribution $p_\theta(u_t\mid u_{<t})$ over the next token. At the first position the prefix contains an agreed beginning marker or is empty according to the tokenizer/model convention.

The probability chain rule factorizes a joint distribution without assuming that adjacent tokens are independent:

\[p_\theta(u_1,\ldots,u_T)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_\theta(u_t\mid u_{<t}).\]

The hinge is conditioning on the full available prefix. Independence would remove that conditioning; autoregression keeps it. Maximum likelihood chooses parameters that assign greater probability to observed sequences. Taking the negative natural logarithm changes the product into an additive loss. Its mean per scored token is

\[\mathcal L(\theta)=-\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_\theta(u_t\mid u_{<t}).\]

The unit is nats per token. Natural-log perplexity is $\exp(\mathcal L)$; it is the reciprocal geometric mean of the probabilities assigned to observed tokens. For probabilities $1/2,1/4,1/8$, the sequence probability is $1/64$, the mean loss is $\log4=1.386294$ nats, and perplexity is 4. Replacing that geometric mean by an arithmetic mean gives a different statistic.

Training commonly uses teacher forcing: the actual preceding tokens are supplied while the model predicts each next token. With a causal mask, many positions can be scored in parallel even though each prediction depends only on its prefix. During generation, the next token must first be chosen by a decoding rule—such as selecting the largest probability or sampling—before it can become part of the next prefix. Parallel training therefore does not make ordinary autoregressive decoding simultaneous.

For the technician's assistant, lowering text loss improves the fit to a declared text distribution. It does not enforce that the newest manual governs, that a cited part exists, or that a proposed act is authorized. Those properties require explicit evidence and application checks. If training includes obsolete procedures, predicting them well can be rewarded by the likelihood objective.

2. Represent a token, then represent its context 10:15

Lecture-derived: word embeddings, ambiguity, recurrent networks, and long short-term memory. Original development: persistent dimensions and a retention calculation.

An embedding maps a discrete token to a real vector. Let $d$ be a positive integer called the model width and let $E\in \mathbb R^{|\mathcal V|\times d}$ be a learned table. Row $E_{u_t}$ represents token $u_t$. A static embedding gives the same token the same initial vector wherever it occurs. The “bank” beside a river and the “bank” financing an installation may therefore begin alike. A contextual representation is a later vector computed using the surrounding permitted tokens; those two occurrences can then differ.

Vector arithmetic can expose regularities in particular trained embeddings. It is not a definition of meaning and does not require every analogy to work. For the assistant, “seal” can name a component or an action. Resolving the intended sense needs the machine identifier and nearby instructions, not a promise that subtracting and adding three vocabulary vectors will produce the right sense.

A recurrent neural network processes a sequence through a hidden state. Define $h_t\in \mathbb R^m$, a vector of width $m$, and an update $h_t=f_\theta(h_{t-1},E_{u_t})$. The state summarizes what earlier steps retain. Unrolling this computation gives a chain of derivatives through time; sensitivity to an old state includes products of the intermediate state Jacobians. If each relevant scalar factor were $0.9$, after 100 steps the factor would be $0.9^{100}\approx 2.66\times10^{-5}$. This illustrative calculation explains how a long chain can attenuate a signal; actual nonlinear matrices can also amplify or rotate it.

A long short-term memory network, or LSTM, adds learned gates governing an additive cell state. In a simplified scalar cell, retention takes the form $c_t=f_t c_{t-1}+i_t g_t$: $f_t,i_t\in[0,1]$ are forget and input gates, $g_t$ is candidate content, and $c_t$ is retained state. A forget gate near one preserves a route for earlier content. It does not guarantee that training discovers the useful route or that finite state preserves every detail of an arbitrarily long manual.

Attention changes the storage-access tradeoff. Instead of forcing all previous content through one recurrent state, it permits direct comparisons with explicitly retained token representations. This helps explain the appeal of transformers without declaring recurrence obsolete. Retaining many tokens creates a different cost problem; compressing them into state creates a different information problem.

3. Derive attention as permitted, normalized information routing 12:23

Lecture-derived: the query/key/value library analogy. Research extension: Vaswani et al., pp. 4–5. Original development: variance, causal leakage, and a fully calculable routing example.

For one layer, collect $n$ token representations in a matrix $X\in \mathbb R^{n\times d}$. Rows are token positions; features run along columns. A self-attention head constructs three different views of the same representations. With positive widths $d_k,d_v$, define learned matrices $W_Q,W_K\in \mathbb R^{d\times d_k}$ and $W_V\in \mathbb R^{d\times d_v}$, and set $Q=XW_Q$, $K=XW_K$, $V=XW_V$. Thus $Q,K$ have $n$ rows of width $d_k$, while $V$ has $n$ rows of width $d_v$. A query asks what information a position seeks; a key provides comparison features; a value supplies the content to combine.

Write $q_i,k_j,v_j$ for rows at positions $i,j$, with $q_i k_j^\top$ the scalar dot product. Let $J_i\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}$ be the permitted key positions for query $i$. Assume $J_i$ is nonempty and every permitted score is finite. The score $s_{ij}$, attention weight $A_{ij}$, and output $o_i$ are

\[s_{ij}=\frac{q_i k_j^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}},\qquad A_{ij}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{e^{s_{ij}}}{\sum_{r\in J_i}e^{s_{ir}}},&j\in J_i,\\0,&j\notin J_i,\end{cases}\qquad o_i=\sum_{j\in J_i}A_{ij}v_j.\]

The weights form a probability distribution over the permitted keys. They are nonnegative and sum to one because the same finite positive denominator normalizes the numerator terms. Consequently each head output lies in the convex hull of its permitted value vectors. This constraint applies before the output projection, residual addition, and other blocks. It does not constrain every final model output to be an average of human meanings.

Why divide by a square root?

The original variance motivation assumes that all query and key coordinates are independent, have mean zero, and have variance one. For coordinate index $r=1,\ldots,d_k$, the product $q_rk_r$ then has mean zero and variance one. Cross-coordinate covariance vanishes under independence, giving $\operatorname{Var}(\sum_r q_rk_r)=d_k$. Dividing the sum by $\sqrt{d_k}$ yields variance one. The assumptions make the calculation legitimate; learned vectors need not satisfy them exactly.

Very large score gaps can make the softmax nearly one-hot. If $a$ is a row of softmax probabilities, differentiating its quotient gives $\partial a_j/\partial s_r=a_j(\mathbf1[j=r]-a_r)$, where $\mathbf1[\cdot]$ is the indicator, equal to one when its condition holds. When a winning probability approaches one and the others approach zero, these derivatives become small. Scaling helps regulate the initial score magnitude; it does not guarantee a particular entropy or successful optimization.

A mask changes information access before normalization

For causal self-attention, $J_i=\{1,\ldots,i\}$. Position $i$'s representation may contain token $u_i$, and its language-model head predicts $u_{i+1}$. The target is shifted by one position. Allowing token $u_{i+1}$ into this representation would leak the answer during training. A mask is therefore a constraint on the function being learned, not merely a way to save computation.

Take an original one-dimensional fixture with $d_k=d_v=1$. All queries equal 1; the three keys are $0,\log3,\log2$; their values are $0,6,12$. At position 1 only the first key is permitted, so the output is 0. At position 2 the exponentials are 1 and 3, giving weights $1/4,3/4$ and output 4.5. At position 3 the exponentials are $1,3,2$, giving weights $1/6,1/2,1/3$ and output 7. Without masking, even position 1 would output 7 by reading future values.

Removing forbidden probabilities after ordinary softmax without renormalization is wrong. For position 2, a full softmax gives the allowed weights $1/6,1/2$, which sum to $2/3$. Zeroing the future entry then produces output 3 rather than 4.5. The denominator must exclude forbidden keys. An entirely forbidden row has no probability distribution; code should reject it or apply a separately specified convention, rather than allow undefined arithmetic to propagate.

For stable computation, subtract the largest permitted score $m_i$ from every permitted score before exponentiation. The common factor $e^{-m_i}$ cancels from numerator and denominator, leaving exactly the same mathematical probabilities. The largest exponential becomes one, avoiding overflow for ordinary finite score ranges. This is a numerical reformulation, not a change to which tokens are permitted.

In the maintenance task, high attention to the old manual's “lockout” token does not certify that the answer follows the latest procedure. Values can encode different content, and later layers can redirect it. If two scalar values both equal 2, attention weights $(0.9,0.1)$ and $(0.1,0.9)$ produce the same output 2. A routing display identifies a computation worth investigating; a claim about decision dependence needs a defined intervention and a measured output.

4. Add order and multiple routes without changing the dimensions 13:21

Unmasked content-only self-attention is permutation equivariant: rearranging the input positions rearranges the output positions in the same way. To see this, let $P\in \mathbb R^{n\times n}$ be a permutation matrix, which reorders rows and satisfies $P^\top P=I$, with $I$ the identity. Replacing $X$ by $PX$ replaces $Q,K,V$ by $PQ,PK,PV$. The scaled score matrix becomes $P(QK^\top)P^\top/\sqrt{d_k}$. Reordering a softmax row's entries merely reorders its probabilities, so its normalized matrix becomes $PAP^\top$. The output is then $PAP^\top PV=PAV$.

This proof assumes no position-dependent mask or positional features. It demonstrates equivariance, not invariance: outputs still move with the tokens. Summing those outputs would be invariant. Without an order signal or mask, such a system cannot recover arbitrary word-order distinctions from a bag of content vectors. “Release pressure before opening” and “open before releasing pressure” can use the same words while specifying different and consequential procedures.

One remedy adds a positional vector $p_i\in \mathbb R^d$ to the token embedding, giving initial row $x_i=E_{u_i}+p_i$. Content and position now jointly enter the query/key comparisons. Learned absolute positions and the original sinusoidal scheme are different parameterizations. Causal masks also break the full permutation symmetry by encoding permitted prefix structure, but this does not make all explicit positional information unnecessary. Modern architectures may instead modify queries and keys using relative or rotary position rules; those are extensions, not an unchanged copy of the original 2017 model.

Multi-head attention uses $h$ separate query/key/value projection triples, where $h$ is a positive integer. Each head produces $O^{(a)}\in \mathbb R^{n\times d_v}$, indexed by $a=1,\ldots,h$. Concatenation joins their feature columns to make an $n\times hd_v$ matrix. A learned $W_O\in \mathbb R^{hd_v\times d}$ projects it back to model width $d$:

\[\operatorname{MHA}(X)=\operatorname{Concat}(O^{(1)},\ldots,O^{(h)})W_O.\]

If $d=8,h=2,d_v=4$, each head returns four features per position, concatenation returns eight, and $W_O$ has shape $8\times8$. Two heads with width eight each would instead concatenate sixteen features and need $W_O\in \mathbb R^{16\times8}$. “Add another head” is not a complete dimensional specification.

Different projections can express different comparison rules, but heads are not required to discover distinct human-labeled roles. Two may be redundant. The assistant might benefit from separately representing part compatibility and temporal authority; deciding that a named head performs either job requires evidence from its actual computation. Architectural capacity is a possibility, not an interpretation certificate.

5. A transformer block is more than an attention average 14:12

The original transformer, PDF pp. 3–6, uses encoder and decoder stacks. An encoder reads an input sequence bidirectionally. A decoder reads its own prefix causally and can also use cross-attention to an encoder's output. In cross-attention the queries come from the current decoder representation, while keys and values come from encoded input. Their sequence lengths can differ. A decoder-only language model omits a separate encoder stack; it is not the complete diagram of the original translation system.

A feedforward sublayer changes features at each position after information has been routed between positions. Let a row $z\in \mathbb R^d$, hidden width $d_f$, matrices $W_1\in \mathbb R^{d\times d_f},W_2\in \mathbb R^{d_f\times d}$, and bias rows $b_1\in \mathbb R^{d_f},b_2\in \mathbb R^d$ be defined. The original version uses the rectified linear unit $\operatorname{ReLU}(r)=\max(0,r)$ coordinatewise:

\[\operatorname{FFN}(z)=\operatorname{ReLU}(zW_1+b_1)W_2+b_2.\]

The same weights apply at each position within one layer; different layers can have different weights. Attention mixes token information, while this sublayer transforms each resulting feature vector. Its nonlinearity prevents collapsing the whole stack into one linear map. Removing it changes the represented function even if the attention matrices look plausible.

A residual connection adds the sublayer output to its input, requiring equal dimensions. For a map $F:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$, the residual output is $z+F(z)$. Its Jacobian is $I+J_F(z)$, where $J_F$ is the matrix of partial derivatives. The identity contributes a direct route through the block. It does not guarantee nonvanishing gradients: if $F(z)=-z$, the total Jacobian is zero. The residual design can help optimization without proving optimization success for arbitrary weights.

Layer normalization computes statistics across a position's features. For coordinates $z_r$, mean $\mu=d^{-1}\sum_r z_r$, variance $\sigma^2=d^{-1}\sum_r(z_r-\mu)^2$, learned scales $\gamma_r$, shifts $\beta_r$, and fixed $\epsilon>0$, it outputs

\[\operatorname{LN}(z)_r=\gamma_r\frac{z_r-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}}+\beta_r.\]

The positive $\epsilon$ prevents division by zero for a constant row. With $z=(1,3)$, $\gamma=(1,1)$, $\beta=(0,0)$, and $\epsilon=1$, the mean is 2, variance is 1, and normalized output is $(-1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2)$. Using a sample-variance denominator $d-1$ would produce a different operation. Learned affine parameters and $\epsilon$ mean that final normalized rows need not have exact zero mean or unit variance.

The original post-normalization block applies $\operatorname{LN}(X+\operatorname{MHA}(X))$, then normalization around the feedforward residual. A pre-normalization block applies normalization before a sublayer and retains the outer residual path. These orderings have different Jacobians. A statement about the identity route before normalization cannot silently be carried through a following normalization as an unchanged identity derivative.

For the technician, successive blocks can combine a part mention with an earlier machine identifier, then combine the resulting representation with a procedure revision. A final linear projection supplies vocabulary logits, and softmax converts those logits to next-token probabilities. This layered computation makes contextual prediction possible; whether it preserves the safety condition is still an empirical and application-level question.

6. Count the resource before extrapolating the capability 14:36

Dense attention compares every permitted query/key pair. Ignoring constant factors, the score product requires order $n^2d_k$ arithmetic, and multiplying weights by values requires order $n^2d_v$. When $h d_k$ and $h d_v$ are proportional to model width $d$, the combined projection work scales as $nd^2$; the feedforward work scales as $ndd_f$. Which term dominates depends on length and width; “quadratic attention” is not a complete cost model for a full language model.

A naive full score matrix has $n^2$ scalar entries per head. At $n=4096$, that is 16,777,216 entries: 32 MiB at two bytes each, before gradients, other heads, and other activations. Doubling $n$ gives four times as many full score entries. A causal triangle contains $n(n+1)/2$ permitted pairs, but an implementation may still allocate a full square matrix.

FlashAttention, PDF p. 1, reorganizes exact attention with tiling and careful transfers between memory levels. It avoids naively materializing the entire attention matrix in high-bandwidth memory. That changes storage and input/output cost while retaining exact dense attention's all-pairs computation. Reduced materialized memory should not be described as removing quadratic pairwise arithmetic.

At decoding time, a key/value cache retains prior projected vectors so they need not all be recomputed at every step. For one new query at prefix length $n$, attention still compares with the retained keys. Cache size grows with retained positions, layers, heads, and key/value widths. A longer supported context is an input capacity specification; correct reasoning over everything in that context is a separate evaluation result.

Few-shot prompting supplies examples in the context; zero-shot prompting supplies a task without such demonstrations. Neither necessarily runs an optimizer. Their success must be tested on cases distinguishable from training exposure. The lecture's discussion of emergent capabilities motivates measurement, but a sudden jump on a thresholded score can reflect smooth underlying probability changes. In our assistant, a token's probability can rise from 0.49 to 0.51 and flip a binary choice without proving a discontinuous new mechanism.

7. Small-data learning makes the evaluation target visible 17:22

Pretraining is an initial phase that fits broad representations and predictive behavior, often starting from random weights. The label describes a place in a training process, not a guarantee of general intelligence. The lecture uses small-data projects to ask what the input distribution contributes when increasing the dataset is difficult.

Baby Scale, PDF pp. 1, 3, 5, trains small language models using transcribed language from individual children's environments and controlled pooled conditions. It reports task-dependent scaling and substantial variation among family datasets. Grammar measurements improve more cleanly than semantic and world-knowledge measurements at these scales. Associations with distributional and interactional features motivate examining composition alongside size. They do not identify one causal property that guarantees a child's or a model's learning outcome.

The task distinction matters for a small maintenance model. A system can improve grammatical agreement and still confuse which valve fits which machine. A fluent repair instruction is a linguistic result; successful identification of the applicable revision is an evidence result; preventing an unsafe operation is a decision result. Their measurements should remain separate until a stated deployment criterion combines them.

A controlled comparison keeps model architecture, tokenizer policy, optimization budget, and evaluation cases explicit. If a “better” corpus is also longer, cleaner, and exposed for more update steps, several explanations remain. A size-matched comparison tests composition more directly, although it still needs replicated training and uncertainty. Sampling more varied conversations could improve coverage without changing the number of distinct technical nouns; a vocabulary-size statistic alone would miss that difference.

Human learning supplies hypotheses, not an automatic equivalence between children and text predictors. Children interact, act, receive multisensory signals, and learn under different developmental constraints. Training on transcripts of a child's environment models one information channel. It does not reproduce the child's whole experience. The practical lesson for the assistant is to measure the bottleneck that matters: language form, technical content, evidence selection, or action judgment.

8. Match exposure before attributing bilingual interference 25:19

Bringing Up a Bilingual BabyLM, PDF pp. 3–4, 6, uses matched synthetic English/Spanish exposures, including speaker-based separation and sentence- or word-level code-switching. Its baseline comparison preserves first-language exposure while adding second-language data. This asks whether adding the other language harms measured first-language performance; it differs from cutting the first language's share within a fixed total.

The distinction can be stated without any neural benchmark. Let a corpus contain $B$ words and English share $w\in[0,1]$. English exposure is $wB$. At fixed $B=100$ million words, moving from $w=1$ to $w=1/2$ halves that exposure. Comparing a 50-million-word English baseline with 50 million English plus 50 million Spanish instead holds English exposure fixed and doubles the total. A performance difference in these designs answers different questions. Words must not be silently relabeled as model tokens: token fragmentation depends on the tokenizer.

The published table also limits a blanket “exposure structure is irrelevant” interpretation. Its English sentence-switching perplexity is 5.26, versus 2.69 for the random English baseline, while syntax and word-similarity measurements show much smaller differences. Those are reported study results, with seed variability, not performance figures for our assistant. Similarity in one metric does not imply equality in every metric or every input regime.

For technical bilingual deployment, match semantic content across languages and test code-switching deliberately. “Do not open” and its Spanish equivalent must preserve negation. A tokenizer that splits technical Spanish terms into many more fragments increases sequence length and changes exposure accounting. Compare loss within each language under a stated tokenizer, then separately score part identification, revision selection, and safety-condition preservation. Cross-tokenizer perplexities are not directly interchangeable measures of bilingual competence.

The experiment concerns controlled statistical learners, not advice about raising children and not a proof that every pair of languages avoids interference. The assistant's training mix can include parallel procedures, inconsistent translations, and unbalanced technical vocabulary. Those differences must be inspected before assigning a failure to multilingualism itself.

9. Allocate internal learning and external access separately 29:15

Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, selects documents relevant to a query and supplies them as additional generation context. The retriever changes which evidence the model can read. It does not by itself update the model's parameters, establish that the document is authoritative, or force the answer to use it correctly. For the assistant, retrieving both manual versions is only the beginning of deciding which version applies.

Singh et al.'s April 2026 v1, pp. 4–5, studies pretraining exposure and retrieval-store size under controlled model/data settings. Here “retrieval tokens” describe the indexed corpus size, not the number appended to each prompt and not an equivalent training-compute budget. The August 2026 v2, pp. 1, 4, 6, distinguishes greater gold-answer likelihood gains for smaller models from greater decision-accuracy gains for larger models in its studied comparisons. The later version is dated research beyond the lecture, not a result attributed retroactively to its slides. No universal model-size rule or fixed tokens-per-parameter threshold follows.

An original allocation model exposes the relevant optimization hinge without borrowing fitted constants. Let $D>0$ be pretraining data and $R>0$ indexed data, both measured in one declared normalized corpus unit. Let $B=D+R$ be a fixed total, and let a toy excess loss be $a/D+b/R$, for constants $a,b>0$. This is a constructed convex example, not the cited paper's empirical scaling law.

\[f(D)=\frac aD+\frac b{B-D},\quad 0<D<B,\qquad f'(D)=-\frac a{D^2}+\frac b{(B-D)^2}.\]

At an interior stationary point, equal marginal loss reductions give $a/D^2=b/R^2$, hence $D/R=\sqrt{a/b}$. Therefore $D^*=B\sqrt a/(\sqrt a+\sqrt b)$. The second derivative $2a/D^3+2b/(B-D)^3$ is positive, and loss diverges at both endpoints, so this is the unique unconstrained minimum. With $B=12,a=4,b=1$, it allocates $D=8,R=4$, yielding loss 0.75. Equal allocation gives $4/6+1/6=0.833333$.

If the generator requires at least $D_{\mathrm{min}}\in(0,B)$ exposure in this toy design, the feasible optimum is $\max(D_{\mathrm{min}},D^*)$; the loss is increasing to the right of its unique minimum. With $D_{\mathrm{min}}=10$, the optimum becomes $D=10,R=2$, loss 0.9. A trained generator's actual ability to use retrieval is empirical; the mathematical lower bound here is a stated design constraint, not a measured biological or universal neural threshold.

The assistant's real allocation also includes indexing, storage, update frequency, prompt length, query volume, and latency. A corpus unit spent on training and one indexed externally do not cost the same. When manuals change frequently, a versioned store can expose the revision without retraining; when a basic skill is absent, supplying more documents may not repair the inability to interpret them. Measure retrieval recall and conditional answer quality separately. A correct passage that reaches the context but is ignored is a different failure from a passage never retrieved.

10. Separate data scheduling from model growth 31:59

A curriculum changes the training distribution over stages. Progressive growth changes the architecture or active parameters over stages. These mechanisms can be combined, but their effects need separate comparisons. Curriculum-Guided Layer Scaling, PDF pp. 1, 3–4, adds randomly initialized layers while retaining existing weights, with a phase training the new parameters before full tuning. Its reported improvements concern the specified models, curriculum, budgets, and downstream tasks. They do not prove that an intuitive easy-to-hard order is best for every model.

Define three training distributions—simple instructions, ordinary procedures, and technical exceptions—and stage weights $w_{s,j}\ge0$ summing to one over category $j=1,2,3$. The expected stage loss is $\sum_j w_{s,j}\mathcal L_j(\theta)$, by total expectation. Changing the weights changes which mistakes contribute to optimization. Calling this merely “presenting the same data in a nicer order” can hide changed frequency or repeated exposure.

Growth also changes computation. If two equal-token stages use respectively 4 and 8 similarly sized blocks, their block-token work is $4M+8M=12M$, where $M$ is the tokens per stage. Training an 8-block model for both stages gives $16M$. This idealized count suggests 25% less block work; it is not a promised 25% runtime reduction because embedding, initialization, optimizer, communication, and hardware utilization costs remain.

For a meaningful comparison, use four conditions: neither curriculum nor growth, curriculum alone, growth alone, and their combination. Hold relevant compute or exposure budgets explicit, and repeat seeds. If the combined method improves procedure reasoning but worsens held-out perplexity, report both. The lecture itself distinguishes that possibility. A system should not discard a useful task-specific improvement solely because one aggregate metric rises, nor suppress the regression because a favored task improves.

The assistant's “easy” examples might use common valve names but omit rare warnings. A curriculum ordered by sentence length could therefore delay the most consequential facts. Difficulty should be defined by a reproducible rule and checked against the training model, rather than assigned by intuition. The four data projects together motivate experiments on composition, matched exposure, external access, and scheduling; they are not a single guarantee that one data strategy dominates.

11. More inference computation changes the procedure, not necessarily the weights 37:24

After broad pretraining, supervised fine-tuning updates parameters on selected input/response pairs. Prompting instead changes the context presented to a fixed model. Retrieval changes available evidence. Search generates and evaluates several candidate continuations. All can adapt behavior, but only some update $\theta$. Treating them as one undifferentiated “post-training” operation hides the resource and persistence questions.

Wei et al., PDF p. 1, studies chain-of-thought prompting with examples that include intermediate reasoning text. Those experiments show improvements on the evaluated tasks. A generated derivation gives a candidate explanation that can be checked; it is not direct access to the model's hidden computation. The assistant may produce the right safety conclusion while supplying a post hoc or incorrect rationale.

Tree of Thoughts, PDF p. 3, organizes partial solutions into a search with candidate generation, state evaluation, and an explicit search rule. This differs from sampling several complete answers and taking the most frequent, commonly called self-consistency. With branching factor $b$ and depth $k$, an exhaustive tree can contain $1+b+\cdots+b^k$ nodes. At $b=3,k=4$, there are 121 nodes. Pruning or a beam changes which nodes are explored and can discard the correct branch; a token budget must therefore specify the search policy.

Program-Aided Language Models, PDF p. 1, uses generated programs to delegate selected operations to an interpreter. For an installation estimate, a calculator can reliably multiply four parts by a declared per-part cost. It cannot establish that four parts are required if the language model extracted the wrong quantity from the manual. Execution checks the implemented computation; evidence checks its inputs and assumptions.

Recursive decomposition and task graphs make dependencies explicit. The valve decision can be decomposed into machine identity, part compatibility, revision authority, isolation procedure, and operator authorization. Some checks can run independently; choosing the procedure depends on identity and revision. A computation graph states these dependencies, while a natural-language narrative can accidentally use a conclusion before its evidence exists.

More candidates help only if selection has useful information. If every candidate repeats an obsolete procedure and the evaluator prizes confidence, extra inference can amplify the error. The assistant should evaluate each candidate against the same authoritative record, expose unresolved assumptions, and stop when required evidence is absent. An inference budget buys computation, not correctness by definition.

12. Preference training changes a policy under stated feedback 40:18

A policy is a conditional distribution over outputs or actions. In language-model preference training, it distributes probability over complete response sequences. Reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, commonly collects preferred/dispreferred pairs, fits a scalar reward model, and optimizes the policy with constraints on departure from a reference policy. Ouyang et al. supplies an early language-model example. A reward reflects the labeling instructions and model fit; it is not automatically the application's true utility.

Let a prompt be $x$, candidate responses be $y_w,y_l$, and a scalar score be $r(x,y)$. A Bradley–Terry preference model assigns the preferred pair probability $\sigma(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l))$, where $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$. The loss for an observed preference is $-\log\sigma(\Delta)$, with margin $\Delta=r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)$. Differentiation gives derivative $\sigma(\Delta)-1$. At $\Delta=0$, loss is $\log2$ and derivative is $-1/2$; increasing the preferred margin decreases loss.

Direct Preference Optimization, or DPO, pp. 3–4, relates a KL-regularized optimal policy to rewards. Write the reference response distribution as $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$, the trained one as $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$, and the positive regularization scale as $\lambda$. Under its preference and optimal-policy assumptions, a reward can be expressed as $\lambda\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$ plus a prompt-dependent normalizing term. Subtracting two responses to the same prompt cancels that term. The DPO margin is therefore

\[\Delta=\lambda\left[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right].\]

The cancellation is why a standalone fitted reward model and an on-policy reinforcement-learning loop are unnecessary for this objective. DPO still needs a reference policy, supported positive response probabilities, and preference data. It is not simply maximizing the preferred response likelihood while ignoring every other quantity. If $\lambda=1$, trained probabilities are $0.6,0.2$, and reference probabilities are $0.2,0.2$, then $\Delta=\log3$, predicted preference probability is $3/4$, and loss is $\log(4/3)=0.287682$. The unused probability mass belongs to other responses; these numbers do not pretend that only two responses exist.

Reinforcement learning from AI feedback, or RLAIF, substitutes model-generated judgments for some human judgments. Lee et al., p. 1, reports comparisons on summarization and dialogue tasks. It does not establish that an AI judge can replace domain validation in every setting. A judge that shares the generator's obsolete manual can reward the same error more consistently. The source of feedback and its independent verification matter as much as its throughput.

DeepSeekMath, pp. 13–14, introduces group relative policy optimization, or GRPO. It samples multiple outputs for one question, scores them, and uses within-group reward statistics to estimate advantages, avoiding a separately learned value baseline in that formulation. “Group relative” does not mean the model necessarily ranks itself. Rewards can come from another model or a task verifier. For rewards $0,0,1,1$, mean is $1/2$, population standard deviation is $1/2$, and normalized advantages are $-1,-1,1,1$. Identical rewards have zero standard deviation and require an explicit implementation rule. A group with no successful output supplies no relative evidence of which failure is correct.

Process supervision evaluates intermediate steps rather than only the final outcome. Lightman et al., p. 1, reports an empirical comparison on mathematical problems. More detailed supervision can locate a mistaken step, but a step scorer can also be fooled. For the assistant, “isolate power” and “confirm zero energy” should be checked individually. A final completion reward alone may fail to distinguish a safe procedure from a shortcut that happens to end successfully.

13. An agent closes a feedback loop around the model 43:12

An agent selects actions using observations and state while pursuing a goal. A language model can propose actions within that system, but it is not the entire agent. The maintenance assistant's state contains the machine identifier, retrieved versions, confirmed observations, outstanding questions, and permitted actions. A tool result becomes a new observation; it should not be treated as a success merely because the tool was called.

A minimal loop reads the current evidence, proposes a next step, checks the proposal's preconditions and permissions, executes an allowed step, and records the actual result. A request to read the newest manual is different from a request to open a physical valve. The former can reduce uncertainty without changing the machine; the latter has a consequence that needs independent authorization.

Refinement critiques and revises a response within one attempt. Reflection can preserve feedback across attempts. Reflexion, PDF p. 1, explicitly uses linguistic feedback and episodic memory rather than weight updates. Improvement in the next attempt therefore need not be parametric learning. Persistent memories can help or harm: storing a mistaken compatibility claim makes it easier to repeat unless the memory also preserves its source and correction state.

Consider three attempts based on the same stale manual. They are correlated even if phrased differently. A majority vote can select the obsolete answer with high apparent agreement. Changing the observation by fetching the applicable revision supplies different information; generating a fourth paraphrase does not. The agent's useful feedback comes from a reliable external check, not from iteration alone.

For a consequential action, the assistant must distinguish proposed, permitted, attempted, and confirmed states. If a read command fails, it has not observed the manual. If an action is denied, it has not completed the operation. A loop that preserves these distinctions can report incomplete work accurately and choose a next step that addresses the actual missing condition.

14. Change the tokenization and objective to handle images 45:21

A vision transformer, or ViT, replaces a text-token table with a map from image patches to vectors. Dosovitskiy et al., PDF p. 3, splits images into patches, linearly embeds them, adds positions, and processes the sequence with transformer blocks. Let an image have height $H$, width $W$, channels $C$, and square patch side $P$ dividing both dimensions. There are $n=HW/P^2$ patches, each flattened to $P^2C$ numbers; a projection maps those numbers into model width $d$.

For $H=W=224,C=3,P=16$, there are $14^2=196$ patch tokens, each containing 768 raw components. A classification token, if used, makes the sequence length 197. Pixel tokenization would give 50,176 positions, and the ratio of dense score entries to 196 patch positions would be $256^2=65,536$, before a classification token. This is token arithmetic, not a measured speedup: patch projection and chosen widths also cost work.

Convolutional neural networks encode locality and parameter sharing in their filters. A basic ViT has less of that particular spatial prior, but it still makes choices about patch layout, position, weight sharing, normalization, and training. “No inductive bias” would be too strong. Inductive bias means assumptions that make some hypotheses easier to represent or learn than others. The useful comparison concerns which biases suit the task and available data, not which architecture has none.

Masked autoencoding hides selected patches and reconstructs them from visible ones. He et al., PDF p. 3, uses an encoder that processes only visible patches and a decoder that receives visible representations and mask tokens. A small numerical image-reconstruction error can reward smooth regions while missing a tiny cracked seal. Mask selection and the reconstruction target determine which evidence is learned. A plausible completed image is not evidence that the missing real part had that appearance.

CLIP, PDF p. 4, trains paired image/text encoders with a symmetric contrastive objective. In a batch of $m$ pairs, let normalized image vectors $a_i$ and text vectors $b_j$ have equal feature width. Their similarity logits are $a_i b_j^\top/\tau$, for temperature $\tau>0$. A row loss rewards the paired text $j=i$ against other texts in the batch; a column loss reverses the retrieval direction. This is matching across modalities, not next-token prediction.

In an original two-pair fixture, give matched similarities 1 and mismatched similarities 0 with $\tau=1$. Each paired probability is $e/(e+1)=0.731059$, and each directional loss is $\log(1+e^{-1})=0.313262$. Reducing temperature sharpens this already correct separation. If the wrong pair has larger similarity, sharpening makes the wrong decision more confident. For the technician, an image-text match can help locate a part but cannot by itself verify model number, internal condition, or installation safety.

15. A scientific prior changes what is hidden and what can be inferred 48:31

Functional magnetic resonance imaging, or fMRI, records a blood-oxygenation-related signal rather than reading individual neural thoughts directly. Resting-state measurements expose correlated fluctuations across brain regions. A predefined functional-network grouping supplies structure for a reconstruction task; it is a scientific modeling choice rather than a partition discovered solely by the transformer.

BrainInterNet, PDF pp. 1, 3–4, masks tokens associated with one network and reconstructs them using the others through cross-attention. Its network-aware design makes the reconstruction's information source explicit. The learned dependence describes predictability and contributions within the trained model and data. It does not establish that one network physiologically causes another's activity, and classification on the study's cohorts is not a prospective clinical diagnosis guarantee.

The regression principle behind reconstruction can be derived independently. Let $Y$ be a real target signal and $Z$ the visible evidence. Suppose $Y$ has finite second moment. For a predictor $f(Z)$, write $\mu(Z)=\mathbb E[Y\mid Z]$. Conditional squared error decomposes as

\[\mathbb E[(Y-f(Z))^2\mid Z]=\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid Z)+(\mu(Z)-f(Z))^2.\]

Expand $Y-f=(Y-\mu)+(\mu-f)$. The cross term has conditional expectation zero because $\mathbb E[Y-\mu\mid Z]=0$. Thus the conditional mean is optimal for squared error, but irreducible conditional variance remains. A predictable target can arise from a common driver or measurement confounder; prediction alone does not identify a causal pathway.

For a numerical check, if hidden signal $Y=Z+\eta$, with zero-mean independent noise $\eta$ of variance 4, then the best predictor is $f(Z)=Z$ and expected squared error remains 4. An apparently imperfect reconstruction can be optimal under its information constraint. Conversely, exceptionally accurate reconstruction deserves checking for leakage, such as information from the target inadvertently entering visible tokens.

The maintenance case has the same information discipline. Masking an entire safety-warning section and predicting it from neighboring pages tests cross-section predictability. It does not certify the reconstructed warning as the authoritative real text. A missing warning should be retrieved or flagged as missing, not replaced by a plausible reconstruction before a dangerous action.

16. Smaller models and additional training repay different limitations 51:59

The lecture's future-facing questions concern small on-device models, domain specialization, computation, self-improvement, and the limits of simply increasing scale. These are research directions. They do not specify a proven route to general intelligence or an inevitable end to scaling.

The assistant makes the tradeoff concrete. An on-device model can keep a copy of manuals available without a network, but storage, updates, latency, and technical accuracy still matter. Fewer stored parameters do not identify how many parameters are active, the precision used, or how much context memory is required. Compare end-to-end response time and energy on the target hardware under the actual query distribution.

Additional training can improve a target behavior while weakening earlier behavior. Catastrophic forgetting describes substantial loss of previously learned performance when learning new information. It must be measured on retained tasks, not inferred from every isolated regression. A post-training procedure that emphasizes pleasant responses might reduce exact citation retention; another procedure might improve both. Save before/after results on fixed cases and distinguish domain-specific change from aggregate mixture change.

A deployment decision needs several outcome dimensions: English and Spanish correctness, authoritative-version selection, refusal on missing evidence, unsafe-proposal rate, latency, and memory footprint. One average score can conceal a rare critical failure. A small model with strict retrieval and a reliable action gate may meet one application contract, while a larger model may still fail it. The contract must be stated before choosing the winner.

17. Define the reference before labeling hallucination 55:05

A hallucinated statement is often described as fluent but false or unsupported content. That description hides a task choice. In summarization, faithfulness to the supplied document can matter even if the document is wrong about the external world. In factual question answering, the external fact may govern. In an agent, a nonexistent interface control is an environment claim. The reference must be explicit before outputs can be compared.

Liu et al., PDF pp. 3–4, proposes a framework with a reference world model, a view, and a conflict policy. It evaluates observable claims using a truth function that may return true, false, or unknown. This is a proposed unifying formalization, not an established inference that any false sentence reveals the model's inaccessible beliefs. Our finite adaptation uses an authoritative reference record $\mathcal W$, visible subset $\mathcal U$, and conflict rule $\mathcal P$; the changed letters avoid colliding with value matrix $V$ and permutation matrix $P$.

Let $C(y)$ be the set of atomic claims in response $y$. Define $T_{\mathcal W,\mathcal P}(x,c)\in\{\mathrm{true},\mathrm{false},\mathrm{unknown}\}$ as the status of claim $c$ for query $x$. In this adaptation, the hallucination label is true if at least one claim is false. An unsupported claim whose status is unknown is separately flagged. “Not proven false” and “established true” are different conclusions.

The manual example makes the policy consequential. For a task asking “summarize revision A,” revision A governs that summary. For “install on this machine using the latest approved procedure,” the applicable approved revision B governs. Saying “revision A permits step S” can be true about the old document but insufficient evidence for doing S now. The same text can be faithful to one source and unsuitable for another task without a change in neural architecture.

The view is what the generator can inspect. If the current manual is omitted, it lacks an important observation; that does not make the current manual nonexistent. Retrieval can improve the view. Conflict policy decides which record controls when evidence disagrees. Confidence calibration changes reported probability, but it does not by itself supply the absent revision or repair a wrong source-of-authority rule.

Consider a response that correctly says the latest manual was used but asserts an unrecorded part compatibility. Its statuses are true and unknown. It may contain no established false claim while still being inadequate for actuation. A separate action rule can require that all required claims be true, lockout be confirmed, and the operator authorize the action. A planning error can occur even when all factual claims are correct: the agent may choose the wrong action order. Evaluation should not relabel every error as a factual hallucination.

For an open world, complete truth may be unavailable. The finite reference check in the lab can establish consistency with its declared record only. It cannot certify a general natural-language answer as globally true. A synthetic benchmark can make the reference, visible subset, and policy fully inspectable; transfer to messy real evidence remains an empirical question.

18. Distinguish stored evidence, context adaptation, and parameter change 1:03:58

The assistant can retain observations in an external store, retrieve selected observations into its next context, or update its parameters through training. These mechanisms have different persistence and failure behavior. A stored document can survive a restarted process without altering $\theta$. An in-context example can alter a response within one session without being kept afterward. An optimizer update can change many responses even if the motivating document is no longer retrieved.

A useful maintenance memory records machine identity, source revision, observation time, confidence or verification status, and what supersedes it. “Valve V fits machine M” without those qualifiers can become dangerous when M is retrofitted. Summarization compresses history but can erase a condition, a negation, or an unresolved conflict. Compression should preserve the information needed for the next decision, not merely shorten the transcript.

Rank-One Model Editing, or ROME, PDF pp. 1, 4, uses interventions to investigate and edit selected factual associations in particular language models. A successful targeted edit is different from a general mechanism for continually acquiring all related knowledge. The paper's model-specific intervention evidence should not be restated as finding a universal “fact neuron.”

If an assistant learns that a part number has changed, dependent compatibility tables, stock records, and procedure references may also need changes. An external relational store can expose those dependencies explicitly. A parameter edit might change the queried answer without updating every logically related answer. Test direct recall, paraphrases, related implications, and unrelated retention separately. A demonstration on one edited fact does not establish coherent propagation.

The lecture advocates more durable learning, while noting an unsettled distinction between parameter updates and in-context adaptation. A system can learn persistently through a changing external memory under a broad system-level definition; calling it parametric learning would be incorrect unless weights change. State which object changed and what persists. “The model learned from yesterday” is insufficient documentation of either mechanism.

19. Interpret a computation and align a decision under different tests 1:08:07

Mechanistic interpretability investigates internal computations and representations, often by examining features and interventions. An activation that correlates with a warning can suggest a hypothesis; changing that activation and measuring a declared output tests a different, causal dependence within the model. Neither alone certifies safe behavior over all new machines.

Alignment asks whether the system's behavior serves intended goals and constraints. A reward can be easier to specify than those goals. If an assistant receives credit for completing installations quickly, skipping lockout might improve that metric while defeating the actual objective. This is reward hacking: optimizing a proxy through behavior that violates its intended purpose. Improving the reward model helps only if its new measurements discriminate the relevant failure.

A written explanation can be checked for validity, but validity and causal faithfulness differ. A correct derivation may have been produced after the model selected its answer by another route. Conversely, a faithful account can expose a mistaken route. To investigate faithfulness, compare controlled changes to the explanation or relevant internal pathway and measure how decisions respond, with care that the intervention changes the computation being tested.

Constitutional AI, PDF p. 1, combines written principles, critiques/revisions, and AI-assisted preference training in its study. A constitution supplies criteria for feedback; it is not a mathematical proof that the resulting policy satisfies them in every situation. Scalable oversight uses automated assistance to expand evaluation, while retaining the problem of evaluating the assistants and their correlated mistakes.

For maintenance, a principle such as “do not proceed without verified isolation” must be connected to machine-state observations and an execution gate. A language-level refusal policy can be tested with ambiguous questions, stale documents, conflicting translations, and pressure to hurry. Shift the task distribution deliberately, since a rule learned on obvious violations may fail when a subtle exception is phrased politely. The checkpoint, observation pipeline, conflict policy, and action gate each deserve their own test.

20. Change the representation target or the sequence state 1:12:18

A world model represents aspects of an environment and predicts consequences or future observations. Next-token prediction can encode environmental regularities, but predicting plausible text is not the same task as correctly predicting the machine's state after an action. A model suitable for planning needs a declared state, action-conditioned transitions, and an error criterion relevant to the decisions.

A joint-embedding predictive architecture, or JEPA, predicts a learned representation of a target from context, rather than necessarily reconstructing every raw input detail. I-JEPA, PDF p. 3, provides an image-based instantiation that uses transformer encoders and a predictor. It therefore illustrates an objective/representation alternative that can still use transformer components; “beyond transformers” does not mean every JEPA removes attention.

Let an encoder $e$ map an observation to a feature vector, and let a predictor $g$ estimate a target feature from context. A simplified squared objective is $\lVert g(e(x_{\mathrm{context}}))-e_{\mathrm{target}}(x_{\mathrm{target}})\rVert_2^2$. If every encoder output and prediction were the same constant vector, the loss would be zero while the features carried no useful distinctions. This representation collapse explains why a target mechanism and anti-collapse design matter. The source's asymmetric encoders and moving-average target are part of that design; the simplified equation alone is not the whole algorithm.

A state-space model, or SSM, compresses history into an evolving state. In a basic discrete linear model, define input $x_t\in \mathbb R^p$, state $s_t\in \mathbb R^m$, output $y_t\in \mathbb R^q$, and fixed matrices $F\in \mathbb R^{m\times m},G\in \mathbb R^{m\times p},H\in \mathbb R^{q\times m}$. Then $s_t=Fs_{t-1}+Gx_t$, $y_t=Hs_t$. Repeated substitution gives $s_t=F^ts_0+\sum_{j=1}^t F^{t-j}Gx_j$, showing exactly how older inputs persist or decay.

For scalar $F=1/2,G=H=1,s_0=0$, inputs $1,0,0$ give outputs $1,1/2,1/4$; inputs $0,0,1$ give $0,0,1$. Order matters. At fixed state dimension, this recurrence takes work proportional to sequence length and does not retain an explicit vector for every past token. That efficiency trades against the information compressed into its state.

Mamba, PDF pp. 3–4, extends structured state-space modeling with input-dependent selection and a hardware-aware computation. Its selective dynamics are richer than the fixed linear example. The absence of naive dense all-pairs attention explains a different scaling route; it does not prove superior performance for every task or remove every serial dependency at decoding. Alternative sequence architectures and predictive objectives answer different questions, and hybrids can combine them.

For the assistant, compare the ability to preserve a warning across a long context, retrieve its exact source, update it when the procedure changes, and predict consequences of a proposed action. Memory-efficient state, better embeddings, or a larger context can each help one part of that problem. None alone establishes the full contract. The overview's durable conclusion is to identify which object each method changes and which evidence would show that the change solves the intended failure.

21. An executable check of information and decision boundaries

This original standard-library program implements the attention formula, conditional loss, toy data allocation, stable preference loss, and a finite evidence/action gate already defined above. Save or open chapter01.py and run python chapter01.py. It reads no files, contacts no services, installs no packages, trains no model, and actuates nothing. Its only effect is printing the finite results and raising an error if a check fails.

Inputs to attention are nonempty rectangular matrices, with equal query/key widths and equal position counts in this deliberately restricted self-attention fixture. Values can have a different feature width. Numeric inputs must be real integers or floats representable as finite Python floats; booleans are excluded. The implementation converts these inputs to binary64 arithmetic and raises ValueError for an unrepresentable input or a nonfinite computed attention score or output. The causal flag must be a boolean. Softmax requires nonempty unique permitted indices. Loss probabilities lie in $(0,1]$; allocation inputs have positive weights and $0<D_{\mathrm{min}}<B$. Cross-attention, trainable projections, batched execution, optimized kernels, and gradients are outside this fixture.

Finite input values do not guarantee that every mathematically positive allocation is numerically distinguishable from an endpoint. For weights 1e308 and 1e-308 with budget 12, rounding can make the computed pretraining share equal to the whole budget, leaving zero indexed data. The executable rejects this result with ValueError rather than violate its positive-budget contract. The continuous optimization still has an interior solution; this error reports the implementation's representability limit. Integer inputs such as 10**1000 likewise receive the declared input error when conversion exceeds float range.

The runnable checks include changing a future value without changing earlier causal outputs, the unmasked permutation relation, overflow-resistant softmax at logits 1000, the geometric-mean loss identity, the constrained allocation boundary, preference loss at extreme margins, and denial of an action with unknown evidence or absent confirmation. These validate the finite implementation and arithmetic. They do not reproduce a transformer benchmark or establish that generated natural-language claims are truthful.

"""Original finite fixtures for CS25 chapter 1; Python 3 standard library."""
import math


def finite_real(value, name):
    """Return a finite binary64 value or a predictable input-contract error."""
    if isinstance(value, bool) or not isinstance(value, (int, float)):
        raise ValueError(name + ': real int/float required; bool is excluded')
    try:
        result = float(value)
    except (OverflowError, ValueError):
        raise ValueError(name + ': value is not representable as a float')
    if not math.isfinite(result):
        raise ValueError(name + ': finite representable value required')
    return result


def matrix(data, name):
    if (not isinstance(data, (list, tuple)) or not data
            or not all(isinstance(row, (list, tuple)) for row in data)):
        raise ValueError(name + ': nonempty rows required')
    width = len(data[0])
    if not width or any(len(row) != width for row in data):
        raise ValueError(name + ': rectangular nonempty matrix required')
    for row in data:
        for value in row:
            finite_real(value, name)
    return width


def softmax(logits, allowed):
    if not isinstance(logits, (list, tuple)) or not logits:
        raise ValueError('finite logits required')
    logits = [finite_real(value, 'logit') for value in logits]
    if (not isinstance(allowed, (list, tuple)) or not allowed
            or any(type(j) is not int or not 0 <= j < len(logits)
                   for j in allowed)
            or len(set(allowed)) != len(allowed)):
        raise ValueError('nonempty unique permitted indices required')
    maximum = max(logits[j] for j in allowed)
    terms = [math.exp(logits[j] - maximum) for j in allowed]
    denominator = sum(terms)
    result = [0.0] * len(logits)
    for j, value in zip(allowed, terms):
        result[j] = value / denominator
    return result


def attention(queries, keys, values, causal=False):
    width = matrix(queries, 'queries')
    if matrix(keys, 'keys') != width:
        raise ValueError('query and key widths must agree')
    value_width = matrix(values, 'values')
    if len(values) != len(keys) or len(queries) != len(keys):
        raise ValueError('this self-attention fixture needs equal row counts')
    if type(causal) is not bool:
        raise ValueError('causal must be a boolean')
    queries, keys, values = [
        [[float(value) for value in row] for row in data]
        for data in (queries, keys, values)]
    weights, outputs = [], []
    for i, query in enumerate(queries):
        logits = [sum(a*b for a, b in zip(query, key)) / math.sqrt(width)
                  for key in keys]
        permitted = list(range(i+1 if causal else len(keys)))
        row = softmax(logits, permitted)
        weights.append(row)
        output = [sum(row[j]*values[j][k] for j in range(len(keys)))
                  for k in range(value_width)]
        outputs.append([finite_real(value, 'attention output')
                        for value in output])
    return weights, outputs


def mean_nll(probabilities):
    if not isinstance(probabilities, (list, tuple)) or not probabilities:
        raise ValueError('nonempty probabilities in (0, 1] required')
    probabilities = [finite_real(p, 'probability') for p in probabilities]
    if any(not 0 < p <= 1 for p in probabilities):
        raise ValueError('nonempty probabilities in (0, 1] required')
    return -sum(math.log(p) for p in probabilities) / len(probabilities)


def allocation(budget, minimum, pretrain_weight=4.0, index_weight=1.0):
    budget, minimum, pretrain_weight, index_weight = [
        finite_real(value, 'allocation input')
        for value in (budget, minimum, pretrain_weight, index_weight)]
    if not 0 < minimum < budget or min(pretrain_weight, index_weight) <= 0:
        raise ValueError('positive weights and 0 < minimum < budget required')
    root_pretrain = math.sqrt(pretrain_weight)
    root_index = math.sqrt(index_weight)
    pretrain = budget*(root_pretrain / (root_pretrain+root_index))
    pretrain = max(minimum, pretrain)
    index = budget-pretrain
    if not 0 < pretrain < budget or not index > 0:
        raise ValueError('positive allocation is not representable')
    return pretrain, index


def preference_loss(margin):
    margin = finite_real(margin, 'preference margin')
    return max(0.0, -margin) + math.log1p(math.exp(-abs(margin)))


def truth(claim, reference):
    if claim not in reference:
        return 'unknown'
    return 'true' if reference[claim] else 'false'


def action_allowed(statuses, lockout, confirmed):
    if (not statuses
            or any(s not in ('true', 'false', 'unknown') for s in statuses)):
        raise ValueError('nonempty known truth labels required')
    if type(lockout) is not bool or type(confirmed) is not bool:
        raise ValueError('lockout and confirmation must be boolean')
    return all(s == 'true' for s in statuses) and lockout and confirmed

22. Six exercises with complete solutions

Exercise 1 · Likelihood, tokenization, and the action criterion

A three-token response receives correct-token probabilities $1/2,1/4,1/8$. Calculate its joint likelihood, mean negative log-likelihood, and perplexity. Another tokenizer splits the final unit into two tokens; their conditional probabilities are $1/4,1/2$, with the first two unchanged. Recalculate. Can you rank the tokenizers' factual reliability using perplexity? Give a concrete reason that the higher-likelihood answer might still fail the maintenance contract.

Solution

The original product is $1/64$; the summed negative log-likelihood is $\log64$. Dividing by three gives $\log4$, and exponentiating gives 4. After splitting, the product is $(1/2)(1/4)(1/4)(1/2)=1/64$ again. Its total loss is unchanged, but mean token loss becomes $\log64/4=(3/2)\log2$, with perplexity $2^{3/2}=2.828427$. Changing the token denominator lowers this metric without changing the sequence likelihood.

These stipulated probabilities show why cross-tokenizer perplexity comparisons require a common unit or carefully matched protocol. Neither metric evaluates the external truth of the response. An obsolete procedure repeated frequently in the corpus may have higher likelihood than a rarely stated revision. The maintenance contract requires applicable authoritative evidence, preserved safety conditions, and authorization in addition to fluent prediction. A tokenizer comparison cannot replace those checks.

Exercise 2 · Causal routing and the wrong denominator

Use the head with queries all 1, keys $0,\log3,\log2$, values $0,6,12$, and widths one. Calculate all causal outputs. Calculate position 2's result if future probabilities are zeroed after full softmax without renormalization. Change the final value to 1200 and calculate position 3's output. State precisely which earlier outputs must remain unchanged and why that does not guarantee correct natural-language behavior.

Solution

The first permitted set is $\{1\}$, giving weight 1 and output 0. The second has exponentials $1,3$, weights $1/4,3/4$, and output $0+18/4=4.5$. The third has exponentials $1,3,2$, weights $1/6,1/2,1/3$, and output $3+4=7$. A full row's weights before masking are $1/6,1/2,1/3$. Removing the last leaves total weight $2/3$, and the position-2 output becomes $6/2=3$. It is not the required normalized attention.

Changing the last value to 1200 gives position 3 output $6/2+1200/3=403$. Positions 1 and 2 remain 0 and 4.5 because their permitted sets exclude position 3; the earlier values and all permitted scores are unchanged. The calculation proves absence of this particular future-value route in this fixed head. Earlier logits could still be wrong for many reasons, and changing an actual input token can affect allowed representations through other permitted paths. A causal information constraint and factual correctness are distinct properties.

Exercise 3 · Order, normalization, and a failed gradient guarantee

Prove unmasked content-only attention equivariance under simultaneous row permutation. Why does the same proof not apply to an unchanged causal mask? Calculate layer normalization of $(1,3)$ with scales 1, shifts 0, and $\epsilon=1$. Give a residual map whose Jacobian is zero, and explain why that counterexample does not make residual connections useless.

Solution

For a permutation matrix $P$, projected matrices become $PQ,PK,PV$. The scaled score matrix becomes $P(QK^\top)P^\top/\sqrt{d_k}$. Row softmax commutes with simultaneous row/column reordering, giving $PAP^\top$. Hence the output is $PAP^\top PV=PAV$, since $P^\top P=I$. An unchanged causal mask is tied to the new position indices. In general it is not the original mask conjugated by $P$, so permitted sets do not transform as required. Reordering tokens can change who is allowed to read whom.

The row mean is 2, population feature variance is 1, and the denominator is $\sqrt{1+1}=\sqrt2$. The result is $(-1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2)$. For residual $z+F(z)$, choose $F(z)=-z$. Its Jacobian is $I-I=0$. This refutes a universal nonvanishing-gradient guarantee for all residual sublayers. It does not refute their usefulness under actual architectures, initialization, and optimization: a direct route exists structurally, but another route can cancel it. A claim about normalization following the sum must additionally include the normalization Jacobian.

Exercise 4 · Two allocation questions and an experimental confounder

Minimize $4/D+1/R$ with $D+R=12,D,R>0$, then impose $D\ge10$. Separately compare a 50-million-word English baseline with 50 million English plus 50 million Spanish, and with a fixed total of 50 million containing equal languages. Which comparison holds English exposure fixed? Design a four-condition experiment to distinguish curriculum from layer growth, and name a metric that could disagree with downstream procedure accuracy.

Solution

Substitute $R=12-D$. Stationarity gives $4/D^2=1/(12-D)^2$; positive denominators yield $D=2(12-D)$, hence $D=8,R=4$. The second derivative $8/D^3+2/(12-D)^3$ is positive, so this is the unique interior minimum, loss $4/8+1/4=0.75$. On $D\ge10$, the function increases from its unconstrained minimum, so the feasible minimum is at $D=10,R=2$, loss 0.9. These constants and normalized corpus units are constructed, not fitted neural scaling parameters.

The 100-million-word bilingual condition preserves 50 million English words and adds 50 million Spanish. The fixed-total 50-million condition has only 25 million English, so reduced first-language exposure is a possible explanation for a regression. Both must state tokenizer and training-update accounting. For curriculum/growth, compare a fixed architecture with random data order, that architecture with the curriculum, progressive growth without the curriculum, and progressive growth with the curriculum. Keep compute or exposure explicit, use held-out fixed tasks and repeated seeds, and inspect whether newly added layer initialization contributes. Per-token held-out likelihood can disagree with correct procedure selection because it scores distributional prediction rather than the application's entire decision rule.

Exercise 5 · Preference probabilities and group-relative feedback

Set $\lambda=1$, trained preferred/dispreferred response probabilities $0.6,0.2$, and both reference probabilities $0.2$. Calculate the DPO margin, implied preference probability, and loss. Show why a prompt-dependent reward normalizer disappears. Calculate normalized advantages for group rewards $0,0,1,1$ using population standard deviation. What changes for four zeros? Can a preferred response still contain an unsafe procedure?

Solution

The log-ratios are $\log3$ and $\log1=0$, giving margin $\log3$. Logistic probability is $1/(1+1/3)=3/4$, and loss is $-\log(3/4)=\log(4/3)=0.287682$. If each response reward is a log-ratio term plus $c(x)$, subtracting two rewards for the same $x$ cancels $c(x)-c(x)$. The cancellation does not eliminate the reference distribution or the preference assumptions.

Group mean is $1/2$; every squared deviation is $1/4$, so population variance is $1/4$ and standard deviation $1/2$. Normalized values are $-1,-1,1,1$. For four zeros, mean and standard deviation are zero; division is undefined. An implementation must explicitly skip the uninformative group, use a defined zero-advantage rule, or state another convention. This illustration does not implement the full clipped GRPO policy update.

A labeler can prefer clarity or confidence while overlooking lockout. A model trained to fit those preferences can then favor the unsafe answer. Preference consistency is evidence about the feedback distribution; independent procedure verification and an execution gate remain necessary. AI-generated preferences do not remove that mismatch merely by making labels cheaper.

Exercise 6 · Evidence, memory, and safe action in an open world

The assistant retrieves an old manual and a revision. Its output correctly identifies the latest revision, falsely says a valve can be opened without lockout, and asserts an unrecorded part compatibility. Assign the three evidence statuses. Suppose it later corrects the lockout statement but compatibility remains unknown and operator confirmation is absent. Is the revised output hallucination-free in the finite reference? Is actuation permitted? Propose a memory schema and an evaluation that distinguishes predictive dependence from causality.

Solution

The statuses are true, false, and unknown. The finite hallucination label is positive because at least one claim contradicts the authoritative reference. After correcting lockout, there may be no false claim, so the finite label can be negative. Unknown compatibility is still unresolved; absence of operator confirmation independently blocks action. “No established contradiction” is not sufficient evidence that every action precondition holds.

Store machine identifier, part number, claim, applicable revision, observation time, source, verification status, and supersession relation. Preserve the old record as historical rather than silently mixing it into the current procedure. Recheck compatibility after a retrofit or newly approved part record, and distinguish memory-store updates from weight training. Test that removing a warning from the visible view leads to a missing-evidence response rather than an invented warning.

For predictive dependence, mask one subsystem's sensor data and measure held-out reconstruction using other data, under a split preventing machine/session leakage. Good prediction can result from a common operating regime that drives both sensors. To test an actual causal claim, specify a controlled intervention and measure the consequence while addressing alternative pathways and confounders; do not treat a large cross-attention weight as that intervention. For maintenance, a validated simulator or an authorized controlled experiment may be needed. The finite chapter lab performs neither physical interventions nor a clinical study.
